\title[The Tammes problem for fifteen points]{The Tammes problem for fifteen points}
\author{\authorlink}
\thanks{2020 {\em Mathematics Subject Classification.} Primary: 52C17; Secondary: 05C10, 52C25, 65G30.\\\indent
{\em Key words and phrases.} Tammes problem; spherical code; contact graph; plane graph enumeration; interval arithmetic; computer-assisted proof.}

\begin{abstract}
We prove that the largest possible minimal angular distance between fifteen points on the unit sphere is $\arccos u\approx 53.65785^\circ$, where $u$ is the root in $(1/2,7/10)$ of $13u^5-u^4+6u^3+2u^2-3u-1$. This value is attained by two configurations described by Buddenhagen and Kottwitz, whose contact graphs are not isomorphic, so the optimum is not unique. As Musin and Tarasov did for thirteen and fourteen points, a written reduction leads to plane graphs with at most fifteen vertices, and an interval computation rules out each of them except near the two known optima, where a local rigidity estimate concludes. The computation records its search trees as certificates, which are replayed. Lean~4 proves every step except the enumeration of the plane graphs and their refutation by the program, which enter the formal proof as hypotheses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

We determine the largest minimal angular distance of fifteen points on the unit sphere. For a finite set $X\subset\Sph$ of at least two points write $\dist(x,y)=\arccos\langle x,y\rangle\in[0,\pi]$ for the angular distance and
\[
\psi(X)=\min_{x\ne y\in X}\dist(x,y),\qquad d_N=\max_{|X|=N}\psi(X);
\]
the maximum exists by compactness. The problem of computing $d_N$ was raised by Tammes \cite{Tam}. It is solved for $N\le 12$ and $N=24$ by Fejes T\'oth \cite{FT43}, Sch\"utte and van der Waerden \cite{SvdW}, Danzer \cite{Dan}, B\"or\"oczky \cite{Bor11}, H\'ars \cite{Har} and Robinson \cite{Rob}, and for $N=13$ and $N=14$ by Musin and Tarasov \cite{MT13,MT14}, with a computer enumeration of contact graphs.

For fifteen points, the bound of Fejes T\'oth \cite{FT43}, $d_N\le\arccos((\cot^2\omega_N-1)/2)$ with $\omega_N=\pi N/(6N-12)$, gives $d_{15}\le56.67150355^\circ$, and Bachoc and Vallentin lowered it to $d_{15}\le55.03^\circ$ by semidefinite programming \cite[Table~5.3]{BV}. The best known configurations reach $53.65785^\circ$. The configuration $\CIII$, with a threefold axis, is the one of Sch\"utte and van der Waerden \cite[Section~17, Fig.~59]{SvdW}; Kottwitz found numerically that a second arrangement, $\CI$, reaches the same minimal distance \cite[Section~5, Fig.~2]{Kot}. Buddenhagen and Kottwitz gave exact coordinates for both and the minimal polynomial of the cosine of their minimal distance \cite[Section~4 and Table~1]{BK}. Both come from one \emph{frame} of eighteen points, a union of three orbits of a dihedral group of order six acting on $\R^3$, in which six points form three close pairs, the \emph{toggle pairs}. Deleting one point of each toggle pair leaves eight configurations of fifteen points, the \emph{frame configurations}, and $\CIII$ and $\CI$ are two of them; Section~\ref{sec:optima} gives their coordinates. Their contact graphs, the graphs of the pairs at minimal distance, are drawn in Figure~\ref{fig:optima}.

Our main result says that these two configurations are optimal.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}\leanmap{Tammes15.conjecture_of_enum_progTreesDom, Tammes15.conjecture_of_enum_killed}\mapnote{the equality $d_{15}=\psi^*$ as \texttt{Tammes15.Conjecture}, from the hypotheses of Theorem \ref{thm:trees}: D2 (Definition \ref{def:dtwo}), the accepted trees of the program and procedures computed by the program (Definition \ref{def:impl}(5)); the second declaration from D2 and D3 (Definitions \ref{def:dtwo} and \ref{def:dthree}); the formal statement does not name $C_1$ and $C_3$}\codemap{code/gp/paper_checks.gp > code/gp/paper_checks.out}
Let $u=0.59260590\ldots$ be the unique root in $(1/2,7/10)$ of
\[
13u^5-u^4+6u^3+2u^2-3u-1,
\]
and let $\psis=\arccos u\approx53.65785^\circ$. Then $d_{15}=\psis$: every set of fifteen points on $\Sph$ contains two points at angular distance at most $\psis$, and the configurations $\CI$ and $\CIII$ have minimal distance $\psis$.
\end{theorem}

The quintic is irreducible over $\Q$ and has a single real root, which lies in $(1/2,7/10)$, where the quintic changes sign, so $u$ is well defined. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} shows that the arrangements of Sch\"utte and van der Waerden and of Kottwitz are optimal. Cohn, de Laat and Leijenhorst note \cite[Section~1 and footnote~1]{CdLL} that there appear to be exactly two optimal codes up to isometry, the first code to be discovered \cite{SvdW} and a modification that moves one point \cite{Kot}. The two are different.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:nonunique}\leanmap{Tammes15.nonunique_of_enum_progTreesDom, Tammes15.nonunique_of_enum_killed, Tammes15.Nonunique.frames_not_iso, Tammes15.PaperSteps.frames_not_isometric, Tammes15.PaperSteps.frames_not_distance_preserving}\mapnote{the first sentence from the hypotheses of Theorem \ref{thm:trees}, and from D2 and D3 (Definitions \ref{def:dtwo} and \ref{def:dthree}) as the second declaration: the maximum $d$ is attained by two configurations whose contact graphs at $\cos d$ are not isomorphic; the statement does not name them, and its proof takes $C_1$ and $C_3$; the non-isomorphism of the contact graphs of $C_1$ and $C_3$ at $u$ is the third declaration, with no hypothesis; the last sentence is the fourth and fifth, with no hypothesis: no isometry of $\mathbb{R}^3$ maps $C_1$ onto $C_3$, and no bijection between their points preserves distances}\codemap{code/gp/contact_graphs.gp > code/gp/contact_graphs.out, code/lean-data/gen_seplt.sh > code/lean-data/gen_seplt.out}
The sets $\CI$ and $\CIII$ are maximal ($\psi=d_{15}$), and their contact graphs are not isomorphic. In particular $\CI$ and $\CIII$ are not isometric.
\end{corollary}

Musin and Tarasov asked \cite[Section~6, Question~2]{MTsurvey}: ``Is it true that there is a unique (up to isomorphism) maximal graph on the sphere for each $N>5$?'' (a maximal graph is the contact graph of a maximal configuration). Corollary~\ref{cor:nonunique} answers this question in the negative for $N=15$. They raise it after noting that for seven points on the square flat torus there are three non-isomorphic maximal graphs \cite{MN}. We do not prove that $\CI$ and $\CIII$ are the only maximal configurations up to isometry.

\subsection*{Method}
We follow Musin and Tarasov \cite{MT13,MT14}. Section~\ref{sec:reduction} proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main} from three statements.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:A} (Section~\ref{sec:structure}): a maximal configuration with the fewest contacts gives a realisation of a plane graph of an explicit finite class, on $12$ to $15$ vertices, whose faces are convex equilateral spherical polygons. The class asks for 3-connectivity, which the enumeration by plantri \cite{BM} requires.
\item Proposition~\ref{prop:B} (Sections~\ref{sec:kill} and~\ref{sec:comp}): every such realisation with edge length in the range of the computation lies within $1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ of a copy of $\CI$ or $\CIII$. Plantri lists $513{,}264{,}771$ graphs in the class, a linear filter on the angles leaves $462{,}703$, and for each of them a branch and prune search in interval arithmetic with outward rounding, which records its search tree as a certificate, refutes every realisation away from the two optima.
\item Theorem~\ref{thm:local} (Section~\ref{sec:local}): every configuration within $1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ of a copy of $\CI$ or $\CIII$ has minimal distance at most $\psis$. The two optimal configurations have different contact graphs, and their subgraphs are realisable at $d=\psis$, so no valid relation refutes them near $\psis$; this quantitative local optimality closes the tie.
\end{enumerate}
The proof is computer-assisted. Lean~4 proves every step of it except two finite statements that the computation checks, the completeness of the enumeration and the refutation of each graph, which enter the formal proof as hypotheses, the second in the form of search trees accepted by procedures computed by the program in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:impl}(5) (Section~\ref{sec:checks}). The formalization reuses the Lean proof of the eight-point case by Kryvonos, Liehr and Taylor \cite{KLT}.

\subsection*{Organisation}
Section~\ref{sec:optima} constructs $\CI$ and $\CIII$, Section~\ref{sec:structure} proves Proposition~\ref{prop:A}, Section~\ref{sec:local} proves Theorem~\ref{thm:local}, and Sections~\ref{sec:kill} and~\ref{sec:comp} prove Proposition~\ref{prop:B}: the refutation test, then the computation. Section~\ref{sec:remarks} lists open questions, and Section~\ref{sec:checks} describes the computations and the formal proof. Appendix~\ref{app:structure} holds the proofs of Section~\ref{sec:structure}, Appendix~\ref{app:programs} the step from the outputs of the programs to the finite statements of Section~\ref{sec:comp}, and Appendix~\ref{sec:leanft} the upper bound $d_{15}\le56.6716^\circ$ that the proof uses, slightly weaker than that of Fejes T\'oth.

\subsection*{Conventions}
Distances on $\Sph$ are angular. A \emph{configuration} is a finite subset of $\Sph$, or a tuple of distinct points when labels matter. For $d\in(0,\pi)$ put
\[
\alpha(d)=\arccos\frac{\cos d}{1+\cos d},\qquad h(d)=\arccos\frac{\cos d}{\cos(d/2)} .
\]
Both are increasing in $d$. The range of the computation is $[\dlo,\dhi]$ with $\dlo=53.65785^\circ$ and $\dhi=56.6716^\circ$. Here $\dlo<\psis$, by $1.3\cdot10^{-7}$ degrees, and $\dhi$ exceeds the bound of Fejes T\'oth by $9.6\cdot10^{-5}$ degrees; all constants derived from $\dlo$ and $\dhi$ are enclosed from exact values (Section~\ref{sec:constants}).

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}\label{sec:reduction}

This section states Propositions~\ref{prop:A} and~\ref{prop:B} and Theorem~\ref{thm:local}, which Sections~\ref{sec:structure} to~\ref{sec:comp} prove, and derives Theorem~\ref{thm:main} from them. It first defines the plane graphs that the computation enumerates and their realisations on the sphere.

\subsection{Plane graphs, cases and realisations}\label{sec:cases}\leanmap{Tammes15.PlaneGraph, Tammes15.RotSys, Tammes15.RotSys.face, Tammes15.InClass, Tammes15.KConnected, Tammes15.FaceSizes, Tammes15.RotSys.Spherical, Tammes15.HexChoice, Tammes15.D2Regions.IsArcDrawing, Tammes15.D2Regions.minorArc, Tammes15.D2Regions.drawingSet, Tammes15.D2Regions.IsRegion, Tammes15.D2Regions.regionSize}\mapnote{plane graphs on the vertex set $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$, the class, the choices of hexagons as lists of darts, and the drawings with their regions}
The graphs of the computation are given combinatorially. A \emph{plane graph} is a simple graph on the vertex set $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ with a \emph{rotation} $\rho$, a permutation of its darts (the ordered pairs of adjacent vertices) that fixes the first vertex of each dart and permutes the darts at each vertex in one cycle. Its \emph{faces} are the cycles of the permutation that reverses a dart and then applies $\rho^{-1}$, and the \emph{size} of a face is its number of darts. A plane graph is \emph{in the class} if its graph is 3-connected (it has at least four vertices, and deleting at most two of them leaves it connected), its degrees lie in $\{3,4,5\}$, its faces have size $3$ to $6$, and $n-E+F=2$ for its numbers $E$ of edges and $F$ of faces.

Let $P$ be a plane graph with $n\le15$ vertices and put $k=15-n$. A \emph{choice of hexagons} is a list of $k$ darts in distinct faces of size $6$. The faces of these darts are its \emph{hexagons}, and the corners $A_0,\dots,A_5$ of a hexagon are the first vertices of its darts in the order of the face, starting from the listed dart. A \emph{case} $(P,H)$ is a plane graph $P$ with at most fifteen vertices and a choice $H$ of hexagons of $P$. Its \emph{points} are the $n$ vertices and $k$ \emph{free points}, one for each dart of $H$; two points are \emph{joined} if they are adjacent vertices.

A \emph{drawing} of a graph with vertex set $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ maps its vertices to distinct points of $\Sph$ such that the ends of each edge are not antipodal, no vertex lies on the minor arc of an edge other than at its ends, and the arcs of distinct edges meet only at vertices. Its \emph{regions} are the connected components of the complement in $\Sph$ of its points and arcs, and the \emph{size} of a region is the number of edges whose arcs lie in its closure. Proposition~\ref{prop:A} produces the following maps from a maximal configuration, and Proposition~\ref{prop:B} confines them near the two optima.

\begin{definition}\label{def:real}\leanmap{Tammes15.Realisation, Tammes15.IsAngular, Tammes15.StrictSupportFace, Tammes15.ocorner}
A \emph{realisation} of a case $(P,H)$ with edge length $d\in(0,\pi/2)$ is a map $x$ from its points to $\Sph$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item adjacent vertices are mapped to points at distance $d$;
\item for every dart $(v,w)$, the angle at $x(v)$ counterclockwise from the arc to $x(w)$ to the arc of $\rho(v,w)$, the \emph{corner} of $(v,w)$, is at most the angle from the arc to $x(w)$ to the arc of any other dart at $v$;
\item every corner lies in $[\alpha(d),\pi)$;
\item for every face of $P$, with vertices $v_0,\dots,v_{m-1}$ in its order, $\det(x(v_i),x(v_{i+1}),x(v_j))>0$ whenever $j\not\equiv i,i+1\pmod m$;
\item any two distinct points are mapped to points at distance at least $d$;
\item each free point is mapped inside the polygon $x(A_0),\dots,x(A_5)$ of its hexagon.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

By (2) and (3), the arcs at a vertex leave it in distinct directions and $\rho$ is the rotation of the drawing of $P$ by $x$; by (4), $x$ maps every face with at least three darts to a convex polygon.

\subsection{Three statements}\label{sec:three}
The first statement, proved in Section~\ref{sec:structure}, turns a maximal configuration into a realisation of a case. It is the structure of maximal contact graphs found by Danzer, B\"or\"oczky and Szab\'o, and Musin and Tarasov, written for fifteen points and our range of $d$.

\begin{mainproposition}\label{prop:A}\leanmap{Tammes15.structured_of_min, Tammes15.realisation_of_structured, Tammes15.D2Regions.planeClass_of_structured}\mapnote{the conclusions of Theorem \ref{thm:structure} in the form of \texttt{Tammes15.Structured} (first declaration), then the realisation (second) and the class of D2 on the drawing (third)}
Suppose $d_{15}\in[\dlo,\dhi]$. Then for some plane graph $P$ in the class with $n$ vertices, $12\le n\le15$, and some choice $H$ of hexagons of $P$, the case $(P,H)$ has a realisation $x$ with edge length $d_{15}$ whose restriction to the vertices is a drawing of the graph of $P$ with all its regions of size at most six.
\end{mainproposition}

The second statement, proved in Section~\ref{sec:comp} with the test of Section~\ref{sec:kill}, says that such a realisation is close to a copy of an optimum. It is a finite statement about the class, which plantri enumerates and the computation refutes graph by graph.

\begin{mainproposition}\label{prop:B}\mapnote{not formalized as one statement; it is the step of the proof of \texttt{Tammes15.reduction} from D2 and D3 to the closeness to a frame configuration}
Let $P$ be a plane graph in the class with $n$ vertices, $12\le n\le15$, let $H$ be a choice of hexagons of $P$, and let $x$ be a realisation of $(P,H)$ with edge length $d\in[\dlo,\dhi]$ whose restriction to the vertices is a drawing of the graph of $P$ with all its regions of size at most six. Then, for $q=\CI$ or $q=\CIII$, labelled as in Table~\ref{tab:frame}, there are an orthogonal map $O$ of $\R^3$ and a bijection $j$ from the points of the case to $\{1,\dots,15\}$ such that $|x(a)-Oq_{j(a)}|\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every point $a$ (Euclidean norm in $\R^3$).
\end{mainproposition}

The third statement, proved in Section~\ref{sec:local}, shows that a configuration that close to an optimum does not beat it.

\begin{maintheorem}\label{thm:local}\leanmap{Tammes15.PaperSteps.local_optimality_frame, Tammes15.local_optimality, Tammes15.PaperSteps.local_optimality_strict, Tammes15.radius_ok, Tammes15.radius_sq_ok, Tammes15.root_lt}\mapnote{as stated, with its equality case, for every labelling of each of the eight frame configurations; the second declaration is the inequality for any labelled configuration of $n$ points with contacts at inner product $u>0$ whose constant $\kappa$ is at least $\kappa_0$, and any $r$ with $\sqrt n\,r\cdot1.01(1+u)\le\kappa_0$ and $nr^2<0.02$; the third gives, when the first condition is strict, that the points are an orthogonal image of the configuration or some contact pair has inner product above $u$}
Let $(p_1,\dots,p_{15})$ be one of the eight frame configurations, labelled. Let $x_1,\dots,x_{15}\in\Sph$, and suppose that $|x_i-Rp_i|\le r=1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for all $i$ and some orthogonal map $R$ (Euclidean norm in $\R^3$). Then $\min_{i<j}\dist(x_i,x_j)\le\psis$, with equality only if $x_i=R'p_i$ for all $i$ and some orthogonal map $R'$.
\end{maintheorem}

The theorem uses only the thirty contact distances of the frame configuration, and it holds for all fifteen points, rattlers included, whatever the contact graph of $(x_i)$.

\subsection{The proof}
The upper bound of Fejes T\'oth is not used; the proof uses the weaker bound $d_{15}\le\dhi=56.6716^\circ$, proved in Appendix~\ref{sec:leanft} (Proposition~\ref{prop:ftbound}). The equalities $\psi(\CI)=\psi(\CIII)=\psis$ are Proposition~\ref{prop:optima}, proved in Section~\ref{sec:optima}.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:optima}, $\psi(\CI)=\psi(\CIII)=\psis$, so $d_{15}\ge\psis$. Suppose that some fifteen points have minimal distance $m>\psis$. The largest minimal distance $d_{15}$ exists by compactness, $d_{15}\ge m>\psis>\dlo$ (Conventions, Section~\ref{sec:intro}), and $d_{15}\le\dhi$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:ftbound}. By Proposition~\ref{prop:A}, some case $(P,H)$ of a plane graph $P$ in the class with $n$ vertices, $12\le n\le15$, has a realisation $x$ with edge length $d_{15}$ whose restriction to the vertices is a drawing with all its regions of size at most six; by Definition~\ref{def:real}(5) any two of its points are at distance at least $d_{15}$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:B}, for $q=\CI$ or $q=\CIII$, some orthogonal map $O$ and some bijection $j$ from the points of the case to $\{1,\dots,15\}$, $|x(a)-Oq_{j(a)}|\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every point $a$. So the points $x_i=x(j^{-1}(i))$ satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:local} for the frame configuration $q$ with $R=O$, and two of them are at distance at most $\psis<d_{15}$, against Definition~\ref{def:real}(5). Hence every set of fifteen points on $\Sph$ contains two points at distance at most $\psis$, and $d_{15}=\psis$.
\end{proof}

This section constructs $\CI$ and $\CIII$ from the eighteen-point frame of Buddenhagen and Kottwitz \cite[Section~4]{BK} and proves that both have minimal distance $\psis$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:optima}\leanmap{Tammes15.Attained.attained, Tammes15.Nonunique.frameC1_sep_lt, Tammes15.Nonunique.frameC3_sep_lt, Tammes15.PaperSteps.optima_four}\mapnote{items (1) and (2) for $C_1$ and $C_3$ as the first declaration, with inner products at most $u$ for the other pairs, the strict inequalities as the second and third; item (3), the last declaration, on sets of points: each element of $\Delta$ maps each frame configuration onto a frame configuration, $C_3$ and $C_1$ are the frame configurations of their toggle points, and their orbits are those of item (3)}\codemap{code/sage/bk15_exact.sage > code/sage/bk15_exact.out, code/lean-data/d4_cut.sage > code/lean-data/d4_cut.out, code/lean-data/regen.sh > code/lean-data/regen.out}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The points of $\CI$ and of $\CIII$ are unit vectors.
\item In $\CI$, and in $\CIII$, exactly thirty pairs of points have inner product $u$, and every other pair has inner product below $u$.
\item The group $\Delta$ acts on the eight frame configurations with two orbits: $\CIII$ and the configuration keeping the other three toggle points form one orbit, the six others the second, which contains $\CI$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, as the elements of $\Delta$ are rotations, items (1) and (2) hold for each of the eight frame configurations, $\psi(\CI)=\psi(\CIII)=\psis$ and $d_{15}\ge\psis$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of item (3)]
The frame is a union of $\Delta$-orbits, and $\Delta$ maps the twelve points of the orbits of $p_V$ and $p_W$ among themselves, so it permutes the toggle pairs and maps each frame configuration onto a frame configuration. The rotation $\zeta$ permutes the toggle pairs cyclically and keeps the side ($\zeta^ip_T$ or $\zeta^{i-1}\chi p_T$) of each chosen point, while $\chi$ maps the side of each pair to the other side of another pair. So the configurations keeping one side entirely form one orbit, and those keeping two points on one side and one on the other form an orbit of size six. This is \lname{Tammes15.PaperSteps.optima_four} in the formalization.
\end{proof}


\begin{proof}[Proof of items (1) and (2)]
Write the coordinates of the points of $\CI$ and $\CIII$ as polynomials in $b$ and $u$ with integer coefficients, of degree at most $3$ in $b$ and $4$ in $u$, divided by $225008$. Let $f$ be the quintic and $g(b,u)$ the polynomial obtained from the quadratic above by putting $y=b^2$, so that $f(u)=g(b,u)=0$. A unit norm or a contact is then an identity $P(b,u)=0$ for a polynomial $P$ with rational coefficients, and it holds because $P=Af+Bg$ for polynomials $A$ and $B$, which a Gr\"obner basis of the ideal generated by $f$ and $g$ provides. For the other pairs, $u$ and $b$ lie in intervals $I_u$ and $I_b$ with rational ends, of widths $10^{-40}$ and $10^{-30}$, and on the box $I_u\times I_b$ the inner product of each such pair, a polynomial in $b$ and $u$ divided by $225008^2$, is below $u$, by interval arithmetic with dyadic rationals. That the box contains a pair of roots follows from the signs of $f$ at the ends of $I_u$ and of $g(\cdot,u)$ at the ends of $I_b$ for every $u\in I_u$, by the intermediate value theorem. Items (1) and (2) are \lname{Tammes15.Attained.attained} in the formalization, with inner products at most $u$ for the other pairs, and two further theorems there give the strict inequalities.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:nonunique}]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:main} both configurations are maximal. By Proposition~\ref{prop:optima}(2), the contact graph of each is the graph of its thirty pairs at inner product $u$; it has thirty edges and degrees $3,3,3$, nine times $4$, and $5,5,5$. In the contact graph of $\CIII$ the three vertices of degree five are $p_V,\zeta p_V,\zeta^2p_V$, pairwise adjacent; in that of $\CI$ they are $p_V$, $\zeta p_V$ and $\zeta\chi p_V$, and only $p_V\zeta p_V$ is an edge among them (Figure~\ref{fig:optima}). So the graphs are not isomorphic, and an isometry between the configurations would induce an isomorphism. This is \lname{Tammes15.nonunique_of_enum_progTreesDom} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

Both contact graphs have $17$ faces: $11$ triangles, $3$ rhombi and $3$ pentagons. They are 3-connected, as a computation on the two graphs checks, so they lie in the class of Section~\ref{sec:cases} and appear in the enumeration of Section~\ref{sec:comp}, and they survive every valid relation there; Theorem~\ref{thm:local} removes them.

\section{The structure of a maximal configuration: proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:A}}\label{sec:structure}\leanmap{Tammes15.Geom.IsCPoly, Tammes15.Geom.Inside, Tammes15.Geom.InClosed, Tammes15.Geom.perim, Tammes15.StrictSupportFace, Tammes15.IsAngular, Tammes15.ocorner}\mapnote{the convex polygons, their insides, closed polygons and perimeters, the rotation of a drawing and its corners}

This section proves Proposition~\ref{prop:A}: some maximal configuration of fifteen points has a contact graph in the class of Section~\ref{sec:cases}, with a realisation. The statements that the proof rests on are stated here with their sources; their proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:structure}.

\subsection{Contact graphs and convex polygons}

\paragraph{Contact graphs.} Let $X$ be a configuration with $\psi(X)=d$. Its \emph{contact graph} $\CG(X)$ has vertex set $X$ and an edge between $x$ and $y$ when $\dist(x,y)=d$; we draw the edge as the minor arc from $x$ to $y$. Two such arcs do not cross \cite[Proposition~3.1]{MT13}. Write $E(X)$ for the number of edges of $\CG(X)$. A configuration $X$ with $|X|=N$ is \emph{maximal} if $\psi(X)=d_N$.

\paragraph{Rattlers.} An isolated vertex of $\CG(X)$ is a \emph{rattler}, and $G'(X)$ denotes $\CG(X)$ without its rattlers, with the induced drawing.

\paragraph{Rotation and corners.} A \emph{dart} is an ordered pair $(v,w)$ of adjacent vertices. A graph drawn on $\Sph$ with minor arcs that leave each vertex in distinct directions has the \emph{rotation} $\rho$ that takes each dart $(v,w)$ to the dart at $v$ whose arc follows $vw$ counterclockwise about $v$, seen from outside the sphere. The \emph{corner} of the dart $(v,w)$ is the angle at $v$, in the tangent plane, from the arc $vw$ counterclockwise to the arc of $\rho(v,w)$ (the full turn $2\pi$ if $v$ has degree one), so that the corners at $v$ sum to $2\pi$ (Figure~\ref{fig:corner}).

\paragraph{Face walks.} The \emph{face walks} are the cycles of the permutation that reverses a dart and then applies $\rho^{-1}$; a face walk turns at each of its vertices through the corner of the dart by which it leaves that vertex, which lies on its left.

Convexity is used in one form throughout. A sequence $A_0,\dots,A_{m-1}$ of $m\ge3$ points of $\Sph$, with indices modulo $m$, is a \emph{convex polygon} if $\det(A_i,A_{i+1},A_j)>0$ for every $i$ and every $j\not\equiv i,i+1$: every vertex other than the ends of a side lies strictly on the left of the great circle of that side. A point $y\in\Sph$ lies \emph{inside} it if $\det(A_i,A_{i+1},y)>0$ for every $i$, and in its \emph{closed polygon} if $\det(A_i,A_{i+1},y)\ge0$ for every $i$. Its \emph{sides} are the minor arcs $A_iA_{i+1}$, and its \emph{perimeter} is $\per A=\sum_i\dist(A_i,A_{i+1})$.

\subsection{The structure theorem}
Proposition~\ref{prop:A} follows from the next theorem, a form for fifteen points and for one maximal configuration of the properties of maximal contact graphs that Musin and Tarasov list \cite[Propositions~3.1 and~3.2]{MT14}, after Danzer \cite{Dan} and B\"or\"oczky and Szab\'o \cite{BS13}. The 3-connectivity of item (2) is the hypothesis of the enumeration by plantri.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:structure}\leanmap{Tammes15.structured_of_min, Tammes15.structure_theorem, Tammes15.exists_contact_of_isGreatest, Tammes15.Geom.rattlers_in_hexagons_proof}\mapnote{items (1) to (4), for $d_{15}$ in any interval $[a,b]\subseteq[d_{lo},d_{hi}]$ with $7a>2\pi$ and $\alpha(b)<2\pi/5$}
Suppose $d_{15}\in[\dlo,\dhi]$. Let $X_0$ be a maximal configuration of fifteen points with $E(X_0)$ minimal among maximal configurations, let $d=d_{15}$, and let $k$ be the number of rattlers of $X_0$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item every corner of $G'(X_0)$ lies in $[\alpha(d),\pi)$, and every vertex of $G'(X_0)$ has degree $3$, $4$ or $5$;
\item the graph of $G'(X_0)$, on $15-k$ vertices, is 3-connected, and $n-E+F=2$ for its numbers $n$ of vertices, $E$ of edges and $F$ of face walks;
\item every face walk of $G'(X_0)$ has $3$ to $6$ darts and is a convex polygon, with all sides of length $d$;
\item every rattler lies inside a face walk of $G'(X_0)$ with six darts, distinct rattlers lie inside distinct face walks, and $k\le3$;
\item distinct points of $X_0$ that are not adjacent in $G'(X_0)$ are at distance greater than $d$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Items (2) to (4) put $G'(X_0)$ in the class of Section~\ref{sec:cases}, and item (1) gives the first relations of the test of Section~\ref{sec:kill}. Item (5) is the definition of the contact graph. The hypothesis on $d_{15}$ holds, by Proposition~\ref{prop:optima} and Proposition~\ref{prop:ftbound} (proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}); in particular $d<\pi/2$. The proof of the theorem assembles the following six statements.

Item (1) comes from the minimality of the number of contacts: a vertex with a corner of at least $\pi$ could be moved off all its contacts. Musin and Tarasov state the degrees for irreducible contact graphs \cite[Propositions~3.2 and~3.3]{MT13}, with proofs in \cite{Dan}; here a configuration with the fewest contacts plays the role of an irreducible one.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:minimal}\leanmap{Tammes15.lemma_minimal, Tammes15.alpha_le_corner, Tammes15.contactCount_lt_of_shift}\mapnote{for every $d\in(0,\pi/2)$ and fifteen points with the fewest contacts among those at pairwise distance at least $d$}
Let $X_0$ be as in Theorem~\ref{thm:structure}. Every corner of $G'(X_0)$ lies in $[\alpha(d),\pi)$ and every vertex of $G'(X_0)$ has degree $3$, $4$ or $5$.
\end{lemma}


Items (2) and (3) rest on the Euler relation for the face walks, their convexity and a bound on their perimeters. Faces with at most $\lfloor2\pi/d\rfloor$ vertices are in \cite[Proposition~3.4]{MT13}, and Musin and Tarasov remark that the faces are convex \cite[Section~3.2]{MT13}. The proof in Appendix~\ref{app:structure} derives the Euler relation, the 2-connectivity and the convexity from the convex hull of the points, without the Jordan curve theorem.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:faces}\leanmap{Tammes15.twoconn_contact, Tammes15.FaceChain.Setup.strictSupportFace, Tammes15.FaceChain.face_perimeter_lt, Tammes15.FaceChain.faceSizes_of, Tammes15.FaceChain.faceconvex_contact, Tammes15.equilateral_angle, Tammes15.rhombus_opposite_angle}\mapnote{with its faces in cone form, with $md<2\pi$ in place of $md=\per F$ and without the closure in an open hemisphere; the first sentence from the first four declarations; the fifth gives the convexity, the sizes and 3-connectivity (Lemma \ref{lem:threeconn}) under the hypotheses of Theorem \ref{thm:structure}; the last sentence is the last two, on a spherical triangle and a spherical rhombus with all sides $d$}
Let $X$ be a configuration with $\psi(X)=d$, where $2\pi/7<d<\pi/2$, such that $G'(X)$ has a vertex and all its corners lie in $(0,\pi)$. Then $G'(X)$ is connected and 2-connected, $n-E+F=2$ for its numbers $n$ of vertices, $E$ of edges and $F$ of face walks, and every face walk of $G'(X)$ passes through distinct vertices, has $m\in\{3,4,5,6\}$ darts, and is a convex polygon with all sides of length $d$ and perimeter $md<2\pi$. A face walk with three darts has its three corners equal to $\alpha(d)$, and in a face walk with four darts opposite corners are equal.
\end{lemma}

The 3-connectivity follows from the convexity of the face walks.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:threeconn}\leanmap{Tammes15.FaceChain.threeconn}\mapnote{for faces in cone form}
Let $G$ be a 2-connected graph on a finite set of points of $\Sph$, with minimum degree at least $3$, whose edges are minor arcs all of the same length, drawn with its rotation, with all its corners in $(0,\pi)$, and whose face walks pass through distinct vertices and are convex polygons. Then $G$ is 3-connected.
\end{lemma}

Item (4) rests on two statements about rattlers. \section{Local optimality: proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:local}}\label{sec:local}

This section proves Theorem~\ref{thm:local}, that $\CI$ and $\CIII$ are optimal within an explicit radius. The computation uses the radius to discard the neighbourhoods of the optima, where no refutation can succeed. The positivity of the constant $\kappa$ below says that no first order motion other than an infinitesimal rotation keeps every contact distance from decreasing, the infinitesimal jamming of Cohn, Jiao, Kumar and Torquato \cite[Section~2]{CJKT}; it amounts to an equilibrium stress with positive entries on the contacts, a proper stress of the tensegrity framework with a strut for each contact in the sense of Roth and Whiteley \cite{RW}. Musin and Tarasov use such stresses to exclude two contact graphs for $N=14$ \cite[Section~4.2]{MT14}, following Connelly \cite{Con}. The constant $\kappa$ makes the argument quantitative, and a certificate, an inequality between quadratic forms checked in exact arithmetic, bounds it.

Fix a frame configuration $p=(p_i)$, labelled, and its set $S$ of thirty contact pairs, $\langle p_i,p_j\rangle=u$ for $ij\in S$. For tangent vectors $t=(t_i)$, $t_i\perp p_i$, put $\Lambda(t)_{ij}=\langle p_i,t_j\rangle+\langle p_j,t_i\rangle$ for $ij\in S$, the first order change of $\langle x_i,x_j\rangle$. Let $T'$ be the orthogonal complement, in the space of tangent vectors, of the three infinitesimal rotations $t_i=\omega\times p_i$; $\Lambda$ vanishes on these. Define
\[
\kappa=\min_{t\in T',\,|t|=1}\ \max_{ij\in S}\Lambda(t)_{ij}.
\]

The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:local} needs a lower bound for $\kappa$ at the two optima, which the next lemma gives by an exact certificate.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:kappa}\leanmap{Tammes15.Kappa.kappaHyp, Tammes15.Kappa.kappa_C1, Tammes15.Kappa.kappa_C3}\codemap{code/lean-data/d1_lp.sage > code/lean-data/d1_lp.out, code/lean-data/d1_kernel.sage > code/lean-data/d1_kernel.out, code/lean-data/gen_close.sh > code/lean-data/gen_close.out, code/lean-data/regen.sh > code/lean-data/regen.out}
For $p=\CI$ and for $p=\CIII$, labelled as in Table~\ref{tab:frame} with their thirty contacts, $\kappa\ge\kappa_0=6.4980\cdot10^{-3}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $|t|^2=\sum_i|t_i|^2$ for $t\in(\R^3)^{15}$, and let $g_1,\dots,g_{18}$ be the linear forms $\langle p_i,t_i\rangle$, $1\le i\le15$, and the three coordinates of $\sum_ip_i\times t_i$. Since $\sum_i\langle\omega\times p_i,t_i\rangle=\langle\omega,\sum_ip_i\times t_i\rangle$, the space $T'$ consists of the $t$ with $g_1(t)=\dots=g_{18}(t)=0$, and the lemma says that each such $t$ has a contact $e\in S$ with $\Lambda(t)_e\ge\kappa_0|t|$.

The proof is a certificate. On $\R\times(\R^3)^{15}$, with coordinates $z=(w,t)$ and $|z|^2=w^2+|t|^2$, put $s_e=w-\Lambda(t)_e$ for $e\in S$ and
\[
F(w,t)=R_c^2w^2-|t|^2-\sum_{e\in S}\lambda_e\,w\,s_e-\sum_{e,f\in S}\mu_{ef}\,s_es_f ,
\]
with rational numbers $R_c^2$, $\lambda_e\ge0$ and $\mu_{ef}\ge0$. Suppose that, for some $\eta\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cert}
F(w,t)\ge-\eta\,|z|^2\quad\text{for all $w$ and all $t\in T'$},\qquad (R_c^2+\eta)\kappa_0^2+\eta<1 .
\end{equation}
Let $t\in T'$, and suppose that $\Lambda(t)_e<\kappa_0|t|$ for every $e\in S$. With $w=\kappa_0|t|\ge0$ every $s_e$ is positive, so $F(w,t)\le R_c^2\kappa_0^2|t|^2-|t|^2$, and \eqref{eq:cert} gives $\bigl(1-(R_c^2+\eta)\kappa_0^2-\eta\bigr)|t|^2\le0$. Hence $t=0$, against $\Lambda(t)_e<\kappa_0|t|=0$.

It remains to choose the data and to prove \eqref{eq:cert}. Let $q$ be $p$ with each coordinate rounded to a multiple of $2^{-100}$. The proof uses only that each coordinate of $p-q$ is at most $\delta=2\cdot10^{-20}$ in absolute value, which follows from the intervals $I_u$ and $I_b$ of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:optima}, through which the coordinates of $p$ are known; let $F_q$ and $g^q_k$ be $F$ and $g_k$ with $q$ in place of $p$. The numbers $R_c^2$, $\lambda_e$ and $\mu_{ef}$ are multiples of $2^{-64}$, and so are the coefficients of eighteen linear forms $m_1,\dots,m_{18}$ in $z$. The quadratic form $F_q-\sum_kg^q_km_k$ on $\R^{46}$ has a matrix $N$ such that $2^{264}N$ has integer entries, and an integer $B$ bounds the sum of the absolute values of the entries of $2^{264}(N+N^{\mathsf T})$; then $|F_q(z)-\sum_kg^q_k(z)m_k(z)|\le\eta_0|z|^2$ with $\eta_0=B/2^{265}$. So the certificate contains no sum of squares: $F_q$ differs from $\sum_kg^q_km_k$ only by the form of matrix $N$, and the forms $g_k$ vanish on $T'$. For $t\in T'$, the forms $g^q_k$ are small: $|g^q_k(t)|\le8\delta|t|$, which bounds $\sum_k|g^q_k(t)m_k(z)|$ by $8\delta K|z|^2$, where $K$ is the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients of the $m_k$. Finally $|p_i|=1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:optima}(1)) gives $|\Lambda_p(t)_e-\Lambda_q(t)_e|\le4\delta|t|$ and $|s_e|\le|w|+2|t|$, hence $|F(z)-F_q(z)|\le\bigl(2\delta\sum_e\lambda_e+4\delta(5+4\delta)\sum_{e,f}\mu_{ef}\bigr)|z|^2$. So \eqref{eq:cert} holds with $\eta=\eta_0+2\delta\sum_e\lambda_e+4\delta(5+4\delta)\sum_{e,f}\mu_{ef}+8\delta K$, provided the last inequality of \eqref{eq:cert} holds. The data are integers: the rounded frame, the multipliers scaled by $2^{64}$, the integer matrix, which is computed from them by exact arithmetic, and $B$; the bound $B$ and the inequality $(R_c^2+\eta)\kappa_0^2+\eta<1$, a comparison of rationals, are checked by exact computation. The data come from a linear program solved in floating point and are checked exactly. This is \lname{Tammes15.Kappa.kappaHyp} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The constant $\kappa$ is defined for any labelled configuration with the set $S$ of contact pairs, and the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:local} applies it to an orthogonal image of $p$, which need not be a frame configuration.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:kappainv}\leanmap{Tammes15.kappaBound_orthogonal}\mapnote{only that a lower bound $\kappa\ge\kappa_0$ passes from $p$ to $Op$}
Let $O$ be an orthogonal map of $\R^3$. If $\kappa\ge\kappa_0$ for $p$ with the set $S$, then $\kappa\ge\kappa_0$ for the configuration $Op=(Op_i)$ with the same set $S$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The map $t\mapsto Ot=(Ot_i)$ sends tangent vectors at $p$ to tangent vectors at $Op$ and preserves $|t|$. It preserves $\Lambda$, since $\langle Op_i,Ot_j\rangle=\langle p_i,t_j\rangle$. It maps $T'$ onto the space $T'$ of $Op$: the vector $t$ is orthogonal to the infinitesimal rotations exactly when $\sum_ip_i\times t_i=0$, because $\sum_i\langle\omega\times p_i,t_i\rangle=\langle\omega,\sum_ip_i\times t_i\rangle$, and $\sum_iOp_i\times Ot_i=\det(O)\,O\sum_ip_i\times t_i$. So the two minima defining $\kappa$ run over the same values, and the constants of $p$ and $Op$ are equal. This is \lname{Tammes15.kappaBound_orthogonal} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:local}]
The orthogonal group is compact, so some orthogonal map $R$ minimises $\sum_i|x_i-Rp_i|^2$; replace $p$ by $Rp$. This changes no inner product and, by Lemma~\ref{lem:kappainv}, keeps the bound $\kappa\ge\kappa_0$ established below; and $\sum_i|x_i-p_i|^2\le15r^2$, since the minimum is at most the value at the map of the hypothesis. Put $v_i=x_i-p_i$ and $s_i=|v_i|^2$. From $|p_i+v_i|=1$ we get $\langle p_i,v_i\rangle=-s_i/2$, so $v_i=t_i-(s_i/2)p_i$ with $t_i\perp p_i$ and $s_i(1-s_i/4)=|t_i|^2$. The first order condition of the minimisation along the maps $e^{\theta\Omega}R$, with $\Omega$ skew symmetric, is $\sum_ip_i\times x_i=0$, that is $\sum_ip_i\times t_i=0$, which says $t\in T'$. Also $|t|^2\le\sum s_i\le15r^2$. For $ij\in S$,
\[
\langle x_i,x_j\rangle-u=\Lambda(t)_{ij}-\frac u2(s_i+s_j)+\langle v_i,v_j\rangle\ge \Lambda(t)_{ij}-(1+u)\max_ks_k ,
\]
using $\langle v_i,v_j\rangle\ge-(s_i+s_j)/2$. Since $s_k\le15r^2<0.02$, we have $s_k\le1.01|t_k|^2\le1.01|t|^2$. So
\[
\max_{ij\in S}\bigl(\langle x_i,x_j\rangle-u\bigr)\ge\kappa|t|-1.01(1+u)|t|^2 ,
\]
which is nonnegative when $|t|\le\kappa/(1.01(1+u))$. This holds because $|t|\le\sqrt{15}\,r$ and $\kappa/(1.01(1+u)\sqrt{15})\ge1.0430\cdot10^{-3}$: by Proposition~\ref{prop:optima}(3) the frame configuration is the image of $\CI$ or $\CIII$ under a rotation of $\Delta$, up to the order of its points, a relabelling of the points and of $S$ does not change $\kappa$, and Lemmas~\ref{lem:kappa} and~\ref{lem:kappainv} give $\kappa\ge\kappa_0$. Hence some pair has $\langle x_i,x_j\rangle\ge u$, that is $\dist(x_i,x_j)\le\psis$, and the inequality is strict when $t\ne0$. If $t=0$, then $s_k=0$ for all $k$ and $x_i=p_i$. This is \lname{Tammes15.PaperSteps.local_optimality_frame} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\section{The refutation test}\label{sec:kill}

This section gives the relations that every realisation of a case satisfies (Sections~\ref{sec:rows} and~\ref{sec:faces}), two tests on a box of values of a realisation, one that refutes the box and one that discards it near an optimum (Section~\ref{sec:glue}), and the search that combines them, with its soundness (Section~\ref{sec:search}). Section~\ref{sec:comp} runs the search on every graph of the class and proves Proposition~\ref{prop:B}.

A realisation is determined, up to isometry, by $d$, the corners at every vertex and, for each free point, the six distances from it to the corners of its hexagon. These are the variables of the test. For a box $B$ of values of these variables (a product of closed intervals), the test tries to prove that no realisation with values in $B$ has minimal distance greater than $\psis$. It uses contractors, which shrink a variable to an interval containing its value in every realisation in $B$, refutations, which prove that $B$ contains no realisation, and one discard, which proves that every realisation in $B$ has minimal distance at most $\psis$.

\subsection{The linear rows}\label{sec:rows}\leanmap{Tammes15.rhombus_rows, Tammes15.rho_concaveOn, Tammes15.rho_strictAntiOn_x, Tammes15.rho_alpha, Tammes15.Ssum_strictMonoOn, Tammes15.alpha_strictMonoOn, Tammes15.equilateral_angle, Tammes15.relSys_of_realisation, Tammes15.vertexSum_of_realisation, Tammes15.vertexSum_realisation, Tammes15.tri_of_realisation, Tammes15.rhombus_of_realisation, Tammes15.tri_of_realisation_proof, Tammes15.rhombus_of_realisation_proof}\mapnote{the rows are fields of the relation system of D3 (Definition \ref{def:relsys})}

\begin{enumerate}
\item $a\in[\alpha(\dlo),\alpha(\dhi)]$, and every corner of a triangle equals $a$;
\item for a rhombus, $a\le x,y\le2a$, $x+y\ge3a$ and $x+y\le \Sigma(\dhi)$ with $\Sigma(d)=4\arctan(1/\sqrt{\cos d})$;

\item[(T3)] $a=\alpha(d)$ and $d=\arccos(\cos a/(1-\cos a))$, both increasing.
\item[(T4)] Rhombus: $y=\operatorname{rh}_d(x)$, $x=\operatorname{rh}_d(y)$ and $\cos d=\cot(x/2)\cot(y/2)$. The map $\operatorname{rh}_d(x)$ is increasing in $d$, since $\cos d\tan(x/2)$ decreases, and $d=\arccos(\cot(x/2)\cot(y/2))$ is increasing in $x$ and in $y$, since the cotangents decrease on $(0,\pi)$.
\item[(T5)] Pentagon $A_0\dots A_4$ with corners $\theta_i$, fan from $A_i$: with $e,b_1$ the base and base angle of the isosceles triangle at $A_{i+1}$ and $f,b_3$ those at $A_{i-1}$ (T1), $\theta_i=b_1+\gamma(d;e,f)+b_3$, $\theta_{i+2}=b_1+\gamma(f;e,d)$ and $\theta_{i-2}=b_3+\gamma(e;f,d)$. The diagonals from $A_i$ lie inside the corner at $A_i$ by convexity.
\item[(T6)] Hexagon $A_0\dots A_5$: the corners $\theta_1,\theta_3,\theta_5$ give the sides of the triangle $A_0A_2A_4$ by (T1), and $\theta_0,\theta_2,\theta_4$ are sums of two base angles and one angle of that triangle; the same with the parities exchanged.
\item[(T7)] Long diagonals: in the chain $A_0A_1A_2A_3$, $\dist(A_0,A_3)\ge d$ holds exactly when $\theta_2\ge b_1+\arccos(\min(1,\cot d\tan(e_1/2)))$, with $e_1,b_1$ from the isosceles triangle at $A_1$; applied to each of the six pairs of adjacent corners of a hexagon in both directions, twelve relations. The bound is non-increasing in $\theta_1$, since $b_1$ decreases and $e_1$ increases with $\theta_1$ by (T1). The short diagonals give $\theta_i\ge\alpha(d)$.
\item[(T8)] Wheel: for a free point $z$ in a hexagon with corners $A_0,\dots,A_5$ and $r_i=\dist(x(z),A_i)$, the triangles $x(z)A_iA_{i+1}$ tile the hexagon, so $\sum_i\gamma(d;r_i,r_{i+1})=2\pi$ and $\theta_i=\gamma(r_{i+1};r_i,d)+\gamma(r_{i-1};r_i,d)$; moreover $d\le r_i\le3d$, because the geodesic from $A_i$ through $x(z)$ leaves the face at a boundary point at boundary distance at most half the perimeter from $A_i$, and $3\dhi<\pi$.
\item[(T9)] Side opposite a known angle: $\cos g=\cos e\cos f+\sin e\sin f\cos G$, used to recover $r_{i\pm1}$ from $r_i$, $d$ and the part of $\theta_i$ between them. The side $g$ is increasing in $G\in[0,\pi]$, and monotone in $e$ with the sign of $\sin e\cos f-\cos e\sin f\cos G$, which is the sign of $\partial g/\partial e$.
\end{itemize}
No relation links vertices of different faces except through the vertex sums and the two tests of Section~\ref{sec:glue}. The maps of (T5) and (T6) are monotone in each corner under conditions that the program tests on the box (Appendix~\ref{sec:model}).

The relations used by the formal proof are collected in one definition. In a plane graph, a face traversed with the face on its left passes each of its corners along one dart, the dart that leaves the corner along the face; we attach the corner to that dart. The corners of a face \emph{read from a dart} $e$ of the face are those at its vertices $A_0,A_1,\dots$ in the order of the traversal, $A_0$ being the first vertex of $e$. For a case $(P,H)$, the corners of a hexagon of $H$ are read from its dart in $H$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:relsys}\leanmap{Tammes15.Assign, Tammes15.RelSys, Tammes15.PentRel, Tammes15.HexRel, Tammes15.HexDiagRel, Tammes15.longDiag, Tammes15.WheelRel}
An \emph{assignment} for a case $(P,H)$ gives a number $d$, a number at each dart of $P$, its corner, and for each free point six numbers $r_0,\dots,r_5$. It satisfies the \emph{relation system} if
\begin{enumerate}
\item every corner lies in $[\alpha(d),\pi)$, and the corners at each vertex sum to $2\pi$;
\item every corner of a triangle equals $\alpha(d)$;
\item in every rhombus, with corners $x_0,\dots,x_3$ read from any of its darts, $x_2=x_0$ and $x_1=\operatorname{rh}_d(x_0)$;
\item in every pentagon, with corners read from any of its darts, the three equations of (T5) for the fan from $A_0$ hold, and the arguments of their arc cosines lie in $[-1,1]$;
\item in every hexagon, with corners read from any of its darts, the three equations of (T6) that give $\theta_0,\theta_2,\theta_4$ hold, with the arguments of their arc cosines in $[-1,1]$, and so do the two inequalities of (T7) for the adjacent corners $\theta_0$ and $\theta_1$;
\item for each free point, the corners of its hexagon and its numbers $r_0,\dots,r_5$ satisfy the wheel relations (T8), with $d\le r_j\le3d$ and the arguments of all their arc cosines in $[-1,1]$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

As the conditions are imposed from every dart, (T6) holds for both parities and (T7) for the twelve ordered pairs of adjacent corners of every hexagon.

\subsection{Gluing, Pair and Local}\label{sec:glue}\leanmap{Tammes15.frame_child_inner, Tammes15.frame_child_eq, Tammes15.frame_child_back, Tammes15.stepM_sub_apply_le, Tammes15.frame_path_enclosure, Tammes15.pair_core, Tammes15.norm_sub_eq_two_sin, Tammes15.local_core, Tammes15.glue_congruent, Tammes15.glue_congruent_proof}\mapnote{the gluing of the formal statement is that of Definition \ref{def:glue}, which differs from that of the program by a reflection (Lemma \ref{lem:verdicts})}
Fix a spanning tree of $P$ by breadth first search from a root $v_0$. Given $d$ and the corners, put $Y(v_0)=(0,0,1)$ with frame $F(v_0)=I$, and for a child $w$ of $v$ put $F(w)=F(v)R_z(\phi)R_y(d)Z$, where $\phi$ is the sum of the corners at $v$ from the reference neighbour of $v$ (a fixed neighbour for the root, the parent in the tree otherwise) to $w$, $R_z,R_y$ are the rotations about the coordinate axes and $Z=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1)$; $Y(w)$ is the third column of $F(w)$. A free point $z$ is placed from a corner $A_i$ of its hexagon at the position $F(A_i)R_z(\phi+\gamma(r_{j};r_i,d))R_y(r_i)(0,0,1)^{\mathsf T}$, where $A_j$ is the neighbour of $A_i$ on the hexagon that precedes it, $\phi$ is the sum of the corners at $A_i$ from its reference neighbour to $A_j$, and $\gamma(r_j;r_i,d)$ is the angle at $A_i$ of the triangle $x(z)A_iA_j$ of (T8); the program tries the six corners and keeps the placement with the least radius $\varrho$ below. Every realisation with these values is congruent to $Y$ or to its mirror image. Over a box, each angle is an interval with midpoint $c$ and radius $\delta$; let $Y_c$ be the configuration built from the midpoints. Since all factors are rotations and $\|R_z(\phi)-R_z(\phi')\|\le|\phi-\phi'|$ in operator norm, every realisation in the box satisfies $|Y(w)-Y_c(w)|\le\varrho(w)$, where $\varrho(w)$ is the sum, over the steps of the tree path to $w$, of the radius of the turning angle and the radius of the step length ($d$ along an edge of the tree, $r_i$ for the step to a free point); the bound grows linearly with the widths.

A \emph{gluing of the program} consists of the spanning tree, the root, the reference neighbour of the root and, for each free point, the corner from which it is placed.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Pair} (a refutation). If two points not joined by an edge satisfy $|Y_c(v)-Y_c(w)|+\varrho(v)+\varrho(w)<2\sin(d_-/2)$, where $d_-$ is the lower end of $d$ in the box, the box contains no realisation, by Definition~\ref{def:real}(5).
\item \emph{Local} (a discard). Its data are the eight frame configurations, each given by a box about each of its fifteen points, a product of three intervals with $40$-digit midpoints, and by its thirty contacts. For a point $z_i$ in each box, an ordered contact $(a,b)$ and a sign $\epsilon=\pm1$, the \emph{target} is the image of $(z_i)$ under $z\mapsto(\langle z,e_1\rangle,\epsilon\langle z,e_2\rangle,\langle z,e_3\rangle)$, where $e_3=z_a$, $e_1$ is the unit vector along $z_b-\langle z_a,z_b\rangle z_a$ and $e_2=e_3\times e_1$. For the exact frame points this places the configuration or its mirror image so that $p_a$ goes to $(0,0,1)$ and $p_b$ into the half plane $y=0$, $x>0$, in $960$ ways. Local fires if for some configuration of the data, ordered contact, sign and bijection $j$ from the fifteen points of $Y$ to $\{1,\dots,15\}$, $|Y_c(v)-T_{j(v)}|+\varrho(v)\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every $v$ and every target $T$ obtained from points $z_i$ in the boxes. Then every realisation in the box is within $1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ of an isometric copy of a frame configuration, point by point, and Theorem~\ref{thm:local} shows that its minimal distance is at most $\psis$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Search, certificates and arithmetic}\label{sec:search}
The search runs on one case $(P,H)$ and one range $[d_1,d_2]$ of $d$, and decides it with the contractors, Pair and Local.

\begin{search}\label{alg:search}\mapnote{the search itself is not formalized; the formal proof reads its trees (Definition \ref{def:procs})}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start from the root box given by the rows of Section~\ref{sec:rows}, $d\in[d_1,d_2]$ and, for the free points, $r_i\in[d_1,3d_2]$ by (T8).
\item Propagate the relations until the box stops shrinking. Optionally apply 3B shaving \cite{Lho}: for each variable, a slice of width $1/8$ of its interval at each end is propagated alone, and removed if the propagation empties it. An empty box is refuted.
\item Test Pair and Local. A box on which Pair holds is refuted, and one on which Local holds is discarded.
\item Otherwise, if all the variables are narrower than $10^{-9}$ radians, stop with the verdict UNRESOLVED; the box is never discarded. Else bisect the variable of largest width relative to its width in the root box, both halves closed, and treat each half from step (2).
\end{enumerate}
The verdict KILLED means that every leaf was refuted or discarded.
\end{search}

The search records its tree in preorder: a split gives the variable and the split point, a leaf the reason of its refutation or discard. The replay rebuilds the boxes from the recorded split points, propagates again and accepts the tree only if every leaf is refuted or discarded again, and only if the leaves cover the root box; a leaf refuted earlier than recorded is accepted. A graph is KILLED on $[d_1,d_2]$ when, for every set of $k$ of its hexagons, the case of one choice of these hexagons is, and a range cut into slices is rounded outward so that consecutive slices overlap.

All arithmetic is on intervals of binary64 numbers, each operation widened by one unit in the last place; the elementary functions are computed from these operations by series with proved remainders (Appendix~\ref{sec:model}).

The soundness of the test is stated in two steps: sound procedures that accept a tree refute every assignment of its range (Proposition~\ref{prop:soundtrees}), and the procedures of the program, restricted to the domain, are sound (Proposition~\ref{prop:sound} in Appendix~\ref{sec:model}). The first step is about the following procedures and trees, with the relation system of Definition~\ref{def:relsys}.

\begin{definition}\label{def:procs}\leanmap{Tammes15.PaperSteps.Procs, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Procs.Sound, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Sol, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Mode, Tammes15.PaperSteps.PVar, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Search.Box, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Search.Tree, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Search.Tree.OK, Tammes15.PaperSteps.LeafKill, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Encl.Covers, Tammes15.PaperSteps.PairC, Tammes15.PaperSteps.TieC, Tammes15.PaperSteps.RootOK}\mapnote{in the formalization the variable of a corner is attached to its dart, and the definitions are stated for all cases at once; the enclosure depends on the graph and $k$, not on the choice of hexagons}
Fix a case $(P,H)$. Its \emph{variables} are $a$, a variable for each corner (the variable $a$ for the corners of a triangle, one variable for two opposite corners of a rhombus), $d$, and the distances $r_0,\dots,r_5$ of each free point; an assignment gives them values, with $a=\alpha(d)$. A \emph{box} gives each variable a closed interval.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Interval procedures} for the case consist of a narrowing for each mode, the first level or the second level with a shaving parameter, which sends each box to a box or to the verdict empty, and an enclosure, which sends a box, a free point and an index $i$ to an interval or to nothing. They are \emph{sound} if, for every assignment with $\dlo\le d\le\dhi$ that satisfies the relation system and every box $B$ that contains its values, each narrowing sends $B$ to a box that contains them, and each interval that the enclosure gives on $B$ contains $\gamma(r_{i-1};r_i,d)$ for the distances of the free point.
\item The \emph{enclosure} over a box of $d$ or of a distance is its interval. The enclosure of a turn of the program has as lower end the larger of the sum of the lower ends of the corner variables it passes and $2\pi$ minus the sum of the upper ends of the other corner variables at the vertex, and as upper end the smaller of the two symmetric sums. The enclosure of the angle that places a free point from its corner $A_i$ is that of its turn plus the interval that the enclosure procedure gives for the point and $i$. A box $B$ \emph{passes the leaf test} if for some gluing of the program there are centres and radii for $d$, for the turn into each vertex other than the root and for the angle and the distance that place each free point, such that each interval of a centre and its radius contains the enclosure of its quantity over $B$, and Pair or Local holds on the configuration $Y_c$ glued from the centres, with the radii $\varrho$ summed along the paths of the tree, in the forms of Section~\ref{sec:glue}, Pair with a lower end $d_-\ge0$ of $d$ in $B$.
\item A \emph{tree} is a leaf, or a split of a variable $v$ at a number $t$ followed by two trees. It is \emph{accepted} from a box $B$ in a mode if the narrowing sends $B$ to empty, or to a box $B'$ that passes the leaf test, or the tree is a split whose two trees are accepted from the halves of $B'$ at $t$, the lower half giving $v$ the upper end $t$ and the upper half the lower end $t$; when $t$ lies below or above the interval of $v$ in $B'$, only the tree on the other side is read, and it must be accepted from $B'$.
\item The \emph{root box} for a range $[d_1,d_2]$ gives $a$ the interval $[\alpha(\dlo),\alpha(\dhi)]$, each corner the interval $[\alpha(\dlo),c]$ with $c=\alpha(\dhi)$ in a triangle, $c=2\alpha(\dhi)$ in a rhombus and $c=\pi$ otherwise, $d$ the interval $[d_1,d_2]$ and each distance $[d_1,3d_2]$. The case has an \emph{accepted tree on} $[d_1,d_2]$ if in some mode some tree is accepted from a box that contains the root box.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The next proposition is what the proof of D3 needs from the search: a tree accepted by sound procedures refutes every assignment of its range, in the sense that Pair fires or Local fires on the configuration that a gluing of the program places from it.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:soundtrees}\leanmap{Tammes15.PaperSteps.progKilled_of_progTrees, Tammes15.PaperSteps.Search.Tree.sound, Tammes15.PaperSteps.glue_enclosure, Tammes15.PaperSteps.leafKill_sound, Tammes15.PaperSteps.root_mem}\mapnote{for every case of a list at once, the first declaration}
Let the procedures be sound, and let the case $(P,H)$ have an accepted tree on $[d_1,d_2]$. Then every assignment with $d\in[d_1,d_2]\cap[\dlo,\dhi]$ that satisfies the relation system has a gluing of the program such that the configuration $Y$ that it glues from the assignment has two points not joined by an edge with $|Y(v)-Y(w)|<2\sin(d/2)$, or Local fires on $Y$ pointwise: for some configuration of the data of Local, ordered contact, sign and bijection $j$, $|Y(v)-T_{j(v)}|\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every point $v$ and every target $T$ obtained from points in the boxes.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The values of the assignment lie in the root box of $[d_1,d_2]$: the corners lie in $[\alpha(d),\pi)$ and sum to $2\pi$ at each vertex, the triangle corners equal $\alpha(d)$ and each rhombus satisfies $y=\operatorname{rh}_d(x)$, all relations of that system, and these give the rows of Section~\ref{sec:rows} for $d\in[\dlo,\dhi]$, those of the rhombi by the argument of that section; $d\le r_i\le3d$ is part of (T8). The rows use constants at $\dlo$ and $\dhi$, which is why $d$ is restricted to $[\dlo,\dhi]$. By induction on the tree, the values lie in a narrowed box $B'$ that passes the leaf test: each narrowing keeps them, by soundness, and the two halves of a split cover the box they split. On $B'$ each angle of the gluing of the assignment lies within its radius of its centre: a turn sums the corners it passes, and the corners at a vertex sum to $2\pi$; the angle of a free point adds to a turn the angle $\gamma(r_{i-1};r_i,d)$, which the enclosure procedure contains. All factors of the gluing are rotations, so $|Y(v)-Y_c(v)|\le\varrho(v)$ for every point, by the bound of Section~\ref{sec:glue}. Pair on the box then gives $|Y(v)-Y(w)|<2\sin(d_-/2)\le2\sin(d/2)$, and Local on the box gives $|Y(v)-T_{j(v)}|\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every target $T$. This is \lname{Tammes15.PaperSteps.progKilled_of_progTrees} in the formalization, for all the cases of a list at once.
\end{proof}

\section{The computation and the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:B}}\label{sec:comp}

This section defines the two finite statements that the computation checks, D2, the completeness of the enumeration, and D3, the refutation of every graph, reports the computation, and proves Proposition~\ref{prop:B} from D2 and D3. Section~\ref{sec:checks} gives the machine, the software and the running times.

\subsection{The statements D2 and D3}\label{sec:leanhyps}
The plane graphs, cases, choices of hexagons and drawings are those of Section~\ref{sec:cases}, and assignments and the relation system those of Definition~\ref{def:relsys}. The statement D2 says that a set of plane graphs contains every graph that Proposition~\ref{prop:A} can produce, up to reflection.

\begin{definition}\label{def:dtwo}\leanmap{Tammes15.EnumComplete, Tammes15.D2Regions.PlaneClass, Tammes15.D2Regions.IsArcDrawing, Tammes15.D2Regions.minorArc, Tammes15.D2Regions.dartArc, Tammes15.D2Regions.drawingSet, Tammes15.D2Regions.IsRegion, Tammes15.D2Regions.Bounds, Tammes15.D2Regions.regionSize, Tammes15.IsAngular, Tammes15.RotSys.IsoRefl}\mapnote{the rotation of the drawing as \texttt{Tammes15.IsAngular}, the condition of Definition \ref{def:real}(2)}
Two plane graphs are \emph{isomorphic up to reflection} if an isomorphism of their graphs carries the rotation of the first to the rotation of the second or to its inverse. A set $\mathcal P$ of plane graphs satisfies \emph{D2} if every plane graph $P$ with $n$ vertices, $12\le n\le15$, whose graph is 3-connected with all degrees in $\{3,4,5\}$ and has a drawing with rotation $\rho$, in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:real}(2), and all its regions of size at most $6$, is isomorphic up to reflection to an element of $\mathcal P$ with $n$ vertices.
\end{definition}

The statement D3 places the points of a case from the values of an assignment by a gluing along a spanning tree, as in Section~\ref{sec:glue} but with exact turns; the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:B} uses it to recover a realisation from its values.

\begin{definition}\label{def:glue}\leanmap{Tammes15.GlueData, Tammes15.GlueData.Valid, Tammes15.GlueData.refD, Tammes15.turnSteps, Tammes15.Assign.turn, Tammes15.GlueData.frame, Tammes15.glueY, Tammes15.stepM, Tammes15.PairFires, Tammes15.LocalFires, Tammes15.rLocal}
A \emph{gluing} consists of a spanning tree of the graph of $P$ rooted at a vertex $v_0$, a dart at $v_0$, and for each free point an index $i\in\{0,\dots,5\}$. The \emph{reference dart} is the given dart at $v_0$, and the dart to the parent at any other vertex. For darts $a,b$ at one vertex let $\tau(a,b)$ be the sum of the corners of $a,\rho a,\dots,\rho^{s-1}a$, where $s\ge0$ is least with $\rho^sa=b$. Given an assignment, put $F(v_0)=I$ and, for a vertex $v$ with parent $w$ and reference dart $a_w$ at $w$,
\[
F(v)=F(w)\,R_z\bigl(\tau(a_w,(w,v))\bigr)\,R_y(d)\,Z ,
\]
with $R_z$, $R_y$ and $Z$ as in Section~\ref{sec:glue}, and $Y(v)=F(v)e_3$. A free point with index $i$, in the hexagon with corners $A_0,\dots,A_5$ and distances $r_0,\dots,r_5$, is placed from $A=A_i$ with reference dart $a_A$:
\[
Y=F(A)\,R_z\bigl(\tau(a_A,(A_i,A_{i+1}))+\gamma(r_{i+1};r_i,d)\bigr)\,R_y(r_i)\,e_3 ,
\]
indices modulo $6$. \emph{Pair fires} on $Y$ if two distinct points not joined by an edge satisfy $|Y(a)-Y(b)|<2\sin(d/2)$. For a set $\mathcal F$ of labelled configurations of fifteen points, \emph{Local fires} on $Y$ with $\mathcal F$ if for some $q\in\mathcal F$, some orthogonal map $O$ and some bijection $j$ from the points to $\{1,\dots,15\}$, $|Y(a)-Oq_{j(a)}|\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every point $a$.
\end{definition}

With the gluing, D3 asks that one of the two tests fire on every assignment that satisfies the relation system.

\begin{definition}\label{def:dthree}\leanmap{Tammes15.Killed, Tammes15.HexChoice, Tammes15.Frame}
Let $\mathcal F$ be a set of labelled configurations of fifteen points. A set $\mathcal P$ of plane graphs satisfies \emph{D3 with $\mathcal F$} if for every $P\in \mathcal P$ with at most fifteen vertices, every choice of hexagons of $P$ and every assignment with $\dlo\le d\le\dhi$ that satisfies the relation system, some gluing makes Pair fire, or Local fire with $\mathcal F$, on the glued configuration.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:B}]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:lists}, the list $\mathcal L$ satisfies D2, and D3 with $\{\CI,\CIII\}$. The graph of $P$ is 3-connected with all degrees in $\{3,4,5\}$, and the restriction of $x$ to the vertices is a drawing of it with rotation $\rho$, in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:real}(2), and with all its regions of size at most six; so by D2, $P$ is isomorphic up to reflection to some $P'\in\mathcal L$ with $n$ vertices. Relabelled along the isomorphism, and replaced by its mirror image $-x$ if the isomorphism reverses the rotation, $x$ is a realisation of $P'$ with a choice of hexagons, with edge length $d$. Its values, $d$, its corners and the distances from its free points to the corners of their hexagons, satisfy the relation system: item (1) of Definition~\ref{def:relsys} by the corners below $\pi$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:alpha}, and the others by Sections~\ref{sec:rows} and~\ref{sec:faces}. By D3, some gluing makes Pair fire, or Local fire with $\{\CI,\CIII\}$, on the configuration $Y$ glued from these values. By induction along the tree, $x=OY$ for an orthogonal map $O$: a child is at distance $d$ from its parent, in the direction that the turn of Definition~\ref{def:glue} gives, and a free point is placed at distance $r_i$ from $A_i$ by the same argument. If Pair fired, two points of $x$ would be at chord distance less than $2\sin(d/2)$, that is at distance less than $d$, against Definition~\ref{def:real}(5). So Local fires: for $q=\CI$ or $q=\CIII$, some orthogonal map $O'$ and some bijection $j'$, $|Y(a)-O'q_{j'(a)}|\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every point $a$. This is a step of the proof of \lname{Tammes15.reduction} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\section{Further remarks}\label{sec:remarks}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The argument controls one maximal configuration, the one with the fewest contacts. We do not know whether $\CI$, $\CIII$ and their mirror images are all the maximal configurations up to isometry. A classification would need, for instance, a proof that every maximal configuration deforms within the maximal ones to one with the fewest contacts, together with the local rigidity of Section~\ref{sec:local}.
\item The enumeration of the class rests on plantri. A formally verified enumeration, such as that of the tame plane graphs in the proof of the Kepler conjecture \cite{NBS}, would turn D2 into a theorem.
\item The formal proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:formal} takes the two finite statements D2 and D3 as hypotheses, and that of Theorem~\ref{thm:trees} replaces D3 by the accepted trees and by procedures computed by the program in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:impl}(5), whose soundness Lean proves. The trees use interval arithmetic on binary64 numbers, which are dyadic rationals, so a checker for them in Lean, with its own arithmetic, would replace the assumption on the program of Section~\ref{sec:notlean} by a finite computation.
\item Musin and Tarasov \cite[Section~6, Question~1]{MTsurvey} also ask whether a maximal graph determines its configuration up to isometry among all configurations with that irreducible contact graph. This stays open for $N=15$; Theorem~\ref{thm:local} only shows that $\CI$ and $\CIII$ are strictly locally optimal, with an explicit radius.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proofs of Section~\ref{sec:structure}}\label{app:structure}

This appendix proves the six statements of Section~\ref{sec:structure} on which Theorem~\ref{thm:structure} rests, and Lemma~\ref{lem:regions}. The proofs are self-contained for our range of $d$.

\subsection{Corners and shifts}
The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:minimal} bounds the corners from below by the next lemma and from above by moving a vertex off its contacts.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:alpha}\leanmap{Tammes15.alpha_le_angle, Tammes15.alpha_bounds}
If $\dist(v,w_1)=\dist(v,w_2)=d<\pi/2$ and $\dist(w_1,w_2)\ge d$, then the angle at $v$ between the arcs $vw_1$ and $vw_2$ is at least $\alpha(d)$, and $\alpha(d)>\pi/3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
With $\theta$ the angle, the spherical law of cosines gives $\cos d\ge\cos\dist(w_1,w_2)=\cos^2d+\sin^2d\cos\theta$, so $\cos\theta\le\cos d(1-\cos d)/\sin^2d=\cos d/(1+\cos d)$. The last quantity is below $1/2$. This is \lname{Tammes15.alpha_le_angle} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:shift}\leanmap{Tammes15.exists_push_dir, Tammes15.shift_config, Tammes15.shift_move}\mapnote{without the closeness of $v'$ to $v$, which Lemma \ref{lem:minimal} does not use}
Let $X$ be a configuration with $\psi(X)=d<\pi/2$ and $v$ a vertex of $G'(X)$. If some corner at $v$ is at least $\pi$, then $v$ can be moved to a point $v'$ with $\dist(v',x)>d$ for every $x\in X\setminus\{v\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $c$ be the unit speed geodesic from $v$ along the bisector of the corner. If the corner exceeds $\pi$, every neighbour direction makes an angle greater than $\pi/2$ with $c'(0)$, so the first variation formula shows that the distance from $c(s)$ to each neighbour increases for small $s>0$. If the corner equals $\pi$, the two neighbours $w_a,w_b$ bounding it lie in opposite directions orthogonal to $c'(0)$, and the right triangle with vertices $v,c(s),w_a$ gives $\cos\dist(c(s),w_a)=\cos d\cos s<\cos d$ since $\cos d>0$; the same holds for $w_b$, and the other neighbours are handled by the first variation. Points of $X$ that are not neighbours of $v$ are at distance greater than $d$ from $v$ and stay so for small $s$. This is \lname{Tammes15.shift_config} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:minimal}]
If a corner at $v$ were at least $\pi$, Lemma~\ref{lem:shift} would give $X'=(X_0\setminus\{v\})\cup\{v'\}$ with $\psi(X')\ge d$, hence $X'$ maximal, and $\CG(X')$ equal to $\CG(X_0)$ without the edges at $v$: fewer edges. The lower bound is Lemma~\ref{lem:alpha}, since two points of $X_0$ are at distance at least $d$. A vertex of degree $1$ or $2$ has a corner at least $\pi$, and a vertex of degree $6$ or more would have corners summing to at least $6\alpha(d)>2\pi$. This is \lname{Tammes15.lemma_minimal} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Faces}
The proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:faces} derives the Euler relation, the 2-connectivity and the simplicity of the face walks from the convex hull of the points (Lemma~\ref{lem:hull} and Corollary~\ref{cor:twoconn}), then their convexity (Lemma~\ref{lem:convex}) and a perimeter bound (Lemma~\ref{lem:polar}). The face walks of a contact graph are simple and wind once around a point; Lemma~\ref{lem:hemi} gives that point, and Lemma~\ref{lem:genus} the count of cycles that Corollary~\ref{cor:twoconn} needs.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hemi}\leanmap{Tammes15.polygon_hemisphere}\mapnote{for closed polygons with sides shorter than $\pi$, whose vertices lie in an open hemisphere}
Let $n_0,\dots,n_L$ be points of $\Sph$, with indices modulo $L+1$, such that $\dist(n_i,n_{i+1})<\pi$ for every $i$ and $\sum_i\dist(n_i,n_{i+1})<2\pi$. Then some $z\in\Sph$ has $\langle z,n_i\rangle>0$ for every $i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The minor arcs $n_in_{i+1}$ form a closed curve of length $\ell=\sum_i\dist(n_i,n_{i+1})<2\pi$. Split it at two points $A,B$ into two arcs of length $\ell/2<\pi$. Then $\dist(A,B)\le\ell/2<\pi$; let $M$ be the midpoint of the minor arc $AB$. Suppose one of the arcs meets the great circle $M^\perp$ at a point $C$, and let $\sigma$ be the half-turn about $M$, so $\sigma(A)=B$ and $\sigma(C)=-C$. The subarc from $C$ to $A$ followed by the image under $\sigma$ of the subarc from $B$ to $C$ (which runs from $A$ to $-C$) joins $C$ to $-C$ with length at most $\ell/2<\pi$, which is impossible. So the curve misses $M^\perp$, and it lies in the open hemisphere $\{\langle x,M\rangle>0\}$, which contains $A$; take $z=M$. This is \lname{Tammes15.polygon_hemisphere} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

For a closed walk $w_0,\dots,w_{m-1}$ of a graph drawn on $\Sph$ with minor arcs, the \emph{pole} of its side $w_iw_{i+1}$ is the unit vector $n_i$ along $w_i\times w_{i+1}$, so that $\langle y,n_i\rangle>0$ on the left of the side. At $w_{i+1}$ the tangent direction of the walk turns to the left by $\pi-\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle on its left there, and since $t\mapsto p\times t$ is an isometry of the tangent plane at $p$, the poles $n_i$ and $n_{i+1}$ are at distance $\pi-\theta$. So if the angles $\theta_c$ on the left of the walk lie in $(0,\pi]$ and satisfy $\sum_c(\pi-\theta_c)<2\pi$, Lemma~\ref{lem:hemi} applies to the poles and gives $z\in\Sph$ with $\det(z,w_i,w_{i+1})>0$ for every $i$.

For a permutation $s$ of a finite set, $\nu(s)$ denotes its number of cycles.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:genus}\leanmap{Tammes15.permutation_cycle_bound_classes}\mapnote{with $c$ the number of classes of any partition of $Z$ such that every function invariant under $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ is constant on each class}
Let $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ be permutations of a finite set $Z$, and let $c$ be the number of orbits of the group that they generate. Then $\nu(\pi_1)+\nu(\pi_2)+\nu(\pi_2\pi_1)\le|Z|+2c$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a permutation $s$ of $Z$ let $A_s$ be the orthogonal map $\phi\mapsto \phi\circ s^{-1}$ of $\R^Z$. Its fixed space $\operatorname{Fix}A_s$ consists of the functions constant on the cycles of $s$ and has dimension $\nu(s)$, and $A_{\pi_2} A_{\pi_1}=A_{\pi_2\pi_1}$. The functions fixed by both $A_{\pi_1}$ and $A_{\pi_2}$ are those constant on the orbits of the group, so $\operatorname{Fix}A_{\pi_1}\cap\operatorname{Fix}A_{\pi_2}$ has dimension $c$. For an orthogonal map $A$, the image of $A-I$ is the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{Fix}A$. Let $\phi\in\operatorname{Fix}A_{\pi_2\pi_1}$ and $\phi'=A_{\pi_1} \phi-\phi$. Then $\phi'$ lies in the image of $A_{\pi_1}-I$, and $\phi'=A_{\pi_1} \phi-A_{\pi_2}(A_{\pi_1} \phi)$ lies in the image of $A_{\pi_2}-I$, so $\phi'$ is orthogonal to $\operatorname{Fix}A_{\pi_1}+\operatorname{Fix}A_{\pi_2}$, a space of dimension $\nu(\pi_1)+\nu(\pi_2)-c$. If $\phi'=0$, then $\phi$ is fixed by $A_{\pi_1}$, hence by $A_{\pi_2}$. So $\phi\mapsto \phi'$ is a linear map from $\operatorname{Fix}A_{\pi_2\pi_1}$ to a space of dimension $|Z|-\nu(\pi_1)-\nu(\pi_2)+c$ whose kernel has dimension at most $c$, which gives the inequality. This is \lname{Tammes15.permutation_cycle_bound_classes} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The hull of a finite set of unit vectors in no closed hemisphere gives a drawn graph whose Euler relation and fan triangles the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:twoconn} uses.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:hull}\leanmap{Tammes15.hullGraph, Tammes15.ExposedPair, Tammes15.exists_angular, Tammes15.hull_step, Tammes15.facetPolar, Tammes15.isFacetPolar_facetPolar, Tammes15.facetPolar_strict, Tammes15.facet_fst_injective, Tammes15.facet_period_ge_three, Tammes15.facet_excess, Tammes15.hull_corner_total, Tammes15.hull_connected, Tammes15.hull_euler, Tammes15.fan_det_pos, Tammes15.cone_unique}
Let $Y$ be a finite set of distinct unit vectors in no closed hemisphere, and $H$ the graph on $Y$ whose edges are the \emph{exposed pairs}: the pairs $\{a,b\}$, $a\ne b$, on which some linear function attains its maximum over $Y$ and nowhere else. Draw $H$ with minor arcs, give it the rotation that takes each dart $(v,w)$ to the dart at $v$ whose arc follows $vw$ counterclockwise about $v$, and call its faces, traversed with the face on the left, its \emph{facets}. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $H$ is connected, and each facet $f$ is a cycle through $k_f\ge3$ distinct points of $Y$, which lie on a plane $\langle\cdot,N_f\rangle=h_f$ with $h_f>0$, while $\langle y,N_f\rangle\le h_f$ for every $y\in Y$; for each side $(q,q')$ of $f$, every other point $y$ of $Y$ on that plane has $\det(q,q',y)>0$;
\item the corners of each facet $f$ lie in $(0,\pi)$, and its \emph{excess} $\operatorname{exc}(f)$, the sum of its corners minus $(k_f-2)\pi$, is positive;
\item $|Y|-E+F=2$, for the numbers $E$ of edges and $F$ of facets of $H$;
\item if $q_0,\dots,q_{k-1}$ are the points of a facet in order, its \emph{fan triangles} $(q_0,q_a,q_{a+1})$, $1\le a\le k-2$, have $\det(q_0,q_a,q_{a+1})>0$, and the open cones $\{\lambda_0q_0+\lambda_1q_a+\lambda_2q_{a+1}:\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\lambda_2>0\}$ of the fan triangles of all facets are pairwise disjoint.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The convex hull $Q$ of $Y$ contains $0$ in its interior, since $Y$ lies in no closed hemisphere, and its vertices are the points of $Y$, which are extreme points of the unit ball. Each face of $Q$ is the convex hull of the points of $Y$ on it, so the exposed pairs are the pairs of ends of the edges of $Q$, and each facet of $Q$ is a convex polygon inscribed in the circle that its plane, which misses $0$, cuts on $\Sph$. The radial projection of the boundary of $Q$ onto $\Sph$ is a homeomorphism. It maps the edges of $Q$ onto the arcs of $H$, which therefore do not cross and leave each point in distinct directions, and each facet of $Q$ onto a convex spherical polygon in an open hemisphere, whose vertices lie on a circle, so that its corners lie in $(0,\pi)$. Around a vertex the edges and the facets of $Q$ alternate, so the facets of $H$ are the boundaries of the facets of $Q$, traversed counterclockwise as seen from outside. This gives (1), the graph of a convex polytope being connected, and (2), since the excess of a facet is the area of its projection. The fan triangles of the facets triangulate the boundary of $Q$ with the orientation of (1), and every ray from $0$ meets the boundary once, which gives (4). For (3), the corners at each point of $Y$ sum to $2\pi$, and $\sum_fk_f=2E$, so $0<\sum_f\operatorname{exc}(f)=2\pi(|Y|-E+F)$ by (2). The reversal of darts, with $E$ cycles, and the inverse of the rotation, with $|Y|$ cycles, generate a group with one orbit, since $H$ is connected, and their product, the face permutation, has $F$ cycles; so Lemma~\ref{lem:genus} gives $E+|Y|+F\le2E+2$. A permutation of the $2E$ darts with $\nu$ cycles has sign $(-1)^{2E-\nu}$, and the signs of the three permutations give $F\equiv E+|Y|$ modulo $2$. So $|Y|-E+F$ is positive, even and at most $2$. Item (3) is \lname{Tammes15.hull_euler} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The corollary passes from the hull to a subgraph of it, such as a contact graph.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:twoconn}\leanmap{Tammes15.twoconn, Tammes15.twoconn_contact, Tammes15.not_closed_hemisphere, Tammes15.contact_exposed, Tammes15.region_excess, Tammes15.region_chi, Tammes15.euler_bound_regions, Tammes15.regions_count, Tammes15.spherical_of_regions, Tammes15.connected_of_regions, Tammes15.region_turn, Tammes15.region_wind, Tammes15.face_walk_injective, Tammes15.kconnected_two}\mapnote{for a finite set of distinct unit vectors and a graph on it without isolated vertices whose edges are exposed pairs, with the face walks of its rotation in place of the faces of the drawing; the second declaration is the case of a contact graph}
Let $V$ be a finite set of distinct points of $\Sph$, and $G$ a graph on $V$ without isolated vertices whose edges are exposed pairs of $V$, drawn with minor arcs, with its rotation, and with all its corners in $(0,\pi)$. Then $G$ is connected and 2-connected, $n-E+F=2$ for its numbers $n$ of vertices, $E$ of edges and $F$ of face walks, and every face walk has at least three darts and passes through distinct vertices.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The corners at a vertex are below $\pi$ and sum to $2\pi$, so every vertex has degree at least $3$ and $G$ has at least four vertices. Let $E_G$ be its number of edges.

\emph{The hull.} The set $V$ lies in no closed hemisphere. Otherwise take $e\ne0$ with $\langle v,e\rangle\le0$ for all $v\in V$, a point $a\in V$ at which $\langle\cdot,e\rangle$ is largest, $m=\langle a,e\rangle\le0$, and the tangent vector $t=e-ma$ at $a$. For a neighbour $w$ of $a$, $\langle t,w\rangle=\langle w,e\rangle-m\langle a,w\rangle\le m(1-\langle a,w\rangle)\le0$. If $t=0$, then $e=ma$ with $m<0$, and $\langle w,e\rangle=m\langle a,w\rangle>m$, against the choice of $a$. So $t\ne0$, the directions of all neighbours of $a$ lie in a closed half plane of the tangent plane, and some corner at $a$ is at least $\pi$. So Lemma~\ref{lem:hull} applies to $V$, with a graph $H$ of $E_H$ edges and $F_H$ facets, and since the edges of $G$ are exposed pairs, $G$ is a subgraph of $H$ with the same arcs. At a vertex $v$, the arcs of $H$ cut each corner of $G$ into corners of facets, whose angles add up to it.

\emph{Regions.} Call two facets adjacent if they share an edge of $H$ that is not an edge of $G$, and call \emph{regions} the classes of facets for the equivalence relation that this generates; let $r$ be their number. A dart of $G$ is a side of the facet on its left, and the corner of $G$ of that dart is cut by the arcs of $H$ into corners of facets of the same region. Let $R$ be a region, with $F_R$ facets and $E_R$ edges of $H$ between two of its facets that are not edges of $G$. The sides of its facets are the $2E_R$ darts of these edges and $c_R$ darts of $G$; let $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_{c_R}$ be the corners of these darts of $G$. The corners of the facets of $R$ add up to $\sum_i\theta_i$, and there are $2E_R+c_R$ of them, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regexc}
\sum_{f\in R}\operatorname{exc}(f)=2\pi(F_R-E_R)-\sum_{i=1}^{c_R}(\pi-\theta_i).
\end{equation}
The left side is positive by Lemma~\ref{lem:hull}(2), and every $\pi-\theta_i$ is positive, so $F_R-E_R\ge1$.

\emph{The count.} Let $\pi_1$ be the permutation of the darts of $H$ that reverses the darts of the edges not in $G$ and fixes the others, and $\pi_2$ the face permutation of $H$, which reverses a dart and then applies $\rho^{-1}$ and so takes a dart to the next dart of its facet. The orbits of the group generated by $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ are the sets of darts of the facets of the regions, $\nu(\pi_1)=E_H+E_G$ and $\nu(\pi_2)=F_H$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:genus} gives $\nu(\pi_2\pi_1)\le E_H-E_G-F_H+2r$. Each edge of $H$ not in $G$ lies between two facets of one region, so the sum of the inequalities $F_R-E_R\ge1$ is $r\le F_H-E_H+E_G$, and $\nu(\pi_2\pi_1)\le r$. The permutation $\pi_2\pi_1$ takes a dart $(u,v)$ of $G$ to the next dart of its facet, and a dart at $v$ that is not in $G$ to the next dart of the facet across its edge, again at $v$. So from $(u,v)$ its iterates run through the darts of $H$ at $v$ clockwise from $vu$, crossing edges not in $G$ only, up to the first dart of $G$, which is the dart that follows $(u,v)$ on its face walk. Hence the darts of $G$ in one cycle of $\pi_2\pi_1$ form one face walk, and the number $F_G$ of face walks is at most $\nu(\pi_2\pi_1)$. Each face walk lies in one region, and each region holds one: from a dart $(v,y)$ of one of its facets, the first dart of $G$ at $v$ clockwise from $vy$ is reached across edges not in $G$ only. So $r\le F_G\le\nu(\pi_2\pi_1)\le r$, and the inequalities of this paragraph are equalities: each region holds exactly one face walk, $r=F_H-E_H+E_G$, $F_R-E_R=1$ for every region, and $F_G=r$, so that $n-E_G+F_G=n-E_H+F_H=2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:hull}(3).

\emph{Connectivity.} The graph $H$ is connected. If $ab$ is an edge of $H$ not in $G$, its two facets lie in one region, and by the last argument $a$ and $b$ are the first vertices of darts of $G$ in that region, hence vertices of its face walk, which joins them in $G$. So $G$ is connected.

\emph{Simplicity.} Let $W=(w_0,\dots,w_{m-1})$ be the face walk of a region $R$, with corners $\theta_i$ at $w_i$. By \eqref{eq:regexc} with $F_R-E_R=1$, $\sum_i(\pi-\theta_i)<2\pi$, and the paragraph after Lemma~\ref{lem:hemi} gives $z\in\Sph$ with $\det(z,w_i,w_{i+1})>0$ for every $i$, indices modulo $m$; moving $z$ slightly, we may assume that $z$ lies in the linear span of no two points of $V$. For two points $p,q$ of one facet of $H$, which are not antipodal since the plane of the facet misses $0$, let $\omega(p,q)\in(-\pi,\pi)$ be the angle from the projection of $p$ to that of $q$ on the plane $z^\perp$, counted counterclockwise as seen from $z$, so that $\omega(p,q)\in(0,\pi)$ exactly when $\det(z,p,q)>0$; the sums below are over such pairs only. Along a closed sequence of points the sum of $\omega$ is $2\pi$ times an integer, its winding number about $z$; the winding number of $W$ is at least $1$. The boundaries of the facets of $R$ consist of the darts of $W$ and of both darts of each edge of $H$ between two facets of $R$ that is not in $G$, so their winding numbers add up to that of $W$. The winding number of the boundary of a facet is the sum of those of its fan triangles, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:hull}(4) a fan triangle winds once about $z$ if $z$ lies in its open cone, $-1$ times if $-z$ does, and $0$ times otherwise. As at most one open cone contains $z$, the winding number of $W$ is $1$. The partial sums of $\omega$ along $W$ then increase strictly from $0$ to $2\pi$, so $w_0,\dots,w_{m-1}$ are distinct. As no vertex has degree one, $m\ge3$, and $W$ passes through $m$ distinct vertices.

\emph{2-connectivity.} If $v$ were a cut vertex, some corner at $v$ would lie between edges to two different components of $G-v$, and the face walk through that corner would leave $v$ into one component and return to $v$ from another, passing through $v$ twice. This is \lname{Tammes15.twoconn} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The convexity of the face walks of a contact graph comes from their simplicity and from corners below $\pi$, by a Wronskian.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:convex}\leanmap{Tammes15.FaceChain.Setup.strictSupportFace, Tammes15.FaceChain.faceconvex_contact, Tammes15.StrictSupportFace}\mapnote{for the faces of a contact graph, in the cone form of Section \ref{sec:eightpoint}: along each face orbit, every vertex that is not an end of a side lies strictly on the left of the great circle of that side; proved from the 2-connectivity through the chain of lemmas on the faces of a contact graph of the eight-point proof}
Let $X$ be a configuration with $\psi(X)=d<\pi/2$ such that $G'(X)$ is 2-connected and all its corners lie in $(0,\pi)$. Then every face walk of $G'(X)$ is a convex polygon.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The edges of $G'(X)$ are exposed pairs of its vertex set: for an edge $xy$ and $N=x+y$, every other vertex $v$ has $\langle v,N\rangle\le2\cos d<1+\cos d=\langle x,N\rangle$. A 2-connected graph has no isolated vertex, so Corollary~\ref{cor:twoconn} and its proof apply. Let $W=(w_0,\dots,w_{m-1})$ be a face walk, indices modulo $m$. By the corollary, $m\ge3$ and the $w_i$ are distinct, and by the paragraph \emph{Simplicity} of its proof there is $z\in\Sph$ with $\det(z,w_i,w_{i+1})>0$ for every $i$, about which $W$ winds once. Let $n_i$ be the pole of the side $S_i=w_iw_{i+1}$, $\Gamma_i$ its great circle and $H_i=\{\langle x,n_i\rangle>0\}$. Then $z\in\bigcap_iH_i$, no $\Gamma_i$ passes through $\pm z$, and we have to show that $w_k\in H_i$ whenever $k\not\equiv i,i+1$.

In polar coordinates $(\varphi,s)$ about $z$, with $s$ the distance to $z$, the longitude increases strictly along each side: along a side with pole $n$, parametrised by $c(t)$ at unit speed, $c\times c'=n$ gives $d\varphi/dt=\langle z,n\rangle/|c-\langle c,z\rangle z|^2>0$. As $W$ winds once about $z$, it meets each meridian once, $W=\{s=r(\varphi)\}$ with $0<r<\pi$, and the longitudes of the vertices satisfy $\varphi_0<\varphi_1<\dots<\varphi_{m-1}<\varphi_0+2\pi$.

Put $w=\cot s$. A great circle with pole $n=\cos\eta\,z+\sin\eta(\cos\varphi_n e_1+\sin\varphi_n e_2)$, where $\cos\eta>0$ and $e_1,e_2$ complete $z$ to an orthonormal basis, has equation $w=-\tan\eta\cos(\varphi-\varphi_n)$, a function $w_i(\varphi)$ in the span $\mathcal T$ of $\cos\varphi$ and $\sin\varphi$, and $H_i=\{w>w_i(\varphi)\}$. Along $W$, $w=U(\varphi)$, where $U=w_i$ on the interval $I_i=[\varphi_i,\varphi_{i+1}]$ of longitudes of $S_i$. At $w_{i+1}$ the corner on the left of the walk is below $\pi$, so $S_{i+1}$ enters $H_i$: the difference $w_{i+1}-w_i$, a nonzero element of $\mathcal T$ vanishing at $\varphi_{i+1}$, changes sign from negative to positive there with positive derivative (nonzero elements of $\mathcal T$ have simple zeros). Thus $U''+U$ is a sum of positive Dirac masses at the vertices.

Fix $j$ and put $g=U-w_j$ and $\sigma(\varphi)=\sin(\varphi-\varphi_{j+1})$. Then $g''+g\ge0$, $g=0$ on $I_j$, and $g'$ jumps by a positive amount at $\varphi_{j+1}$. The Wronskian $\Omega=g'\sigma-g\sigma'$ satisfies $\Omega'=(g''+g)\sigma\ge0$ on $J=(\varphi_{j+1},\varphi_{j+1}+\pi)$, where $\sigma>0$, and $\Omega$ tends to $0$ at $\varphi_{j+1}^+$, since $g$ and $\sigma$ vanish there; hence $(g/\sigma)'=\Omega/\sigma^2\ge0$ on $J$. As $g/\sigma$ tends to the positive jump of $g'$ at $\varphi_{j+1}^+$, we get $g/\sigma>0$, so $g>0$ on $J$. In the same way $g>0$ on $(\varphi_j-\pi,\varphi_j)$. These two intervals and $I_j$ cover the circle, so $U\ge w_j$ everywhere, with equality exactly on $I_j$. A vertex $w_k$ with $k\not\equiv j,j+1$ has its longitude outside $I_j$, so $\cot s=U(\varphi_k)>w_j(\varphi_k)$ at $w_k$, that is $w_k\in H_j$. This is \lname{Tammes15.FaceChain.Setup.strictSupportFace} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The perimeter bound gives the sizes of the face walks in Lemma~\ref{lem:faces}, and its monotonicity serves Proposition~\ref{prop:onehex}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:polar}\leanmap{Tammes15.Geom.perim_mono, Tammes15.FaceChain.face_perimeter_lt, Tammes15.Vendor.EM8.SquareAntiprismVerification.StrictSphericalPolygon.perimeter_lt_two_pi, Tammes15.Vendor.EM8.SquareAntiprismVerification.StrictSphericalPolygon.polar_angle, Tammes15.Geom.volume_lune, Tammes15.Geom.lintegral_cnt, Tammes15.Geom.IsCPoly.triple_pos, Tammes15.Geom.IsCPoly.chg_le_two, Tammes15.Geom.IsCPoly.cone_of_inClosed}\mapnote{only the bound $md<2\pi$ for the faces of Lemma \ref{lem:faces}, and the monotonicity for polygons in cone form, the latter by a formula of Crofton}
Let $A$ be a convex polygon. Then $\per A<2\pi$. If $B$ is a convex polygon whose vertices lie in the closed polygon of $A$, then $\per B\le\per A$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $m$ be the number of vertices of $A$, $\ell_i=\dist(A_i,A_{i+1})$, and $n_i$ the pole of the side $A_iA_{i+1}$. For vectors $a,b,c,e\in\R^3$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:four}
\det(b,c,e)\,a-\det(a,c,e)\,b+\det(a,b,e)\,c-\det(a,b,c)\,e=0 .
\end{equation}
A convex polygon has $\det(A_i,A_j,A_k)>0$ whenever $i<j<k<i+m$. For $j=i+1$ this is the definition; for $j>i+1$, the pairing of \eqref{eq:four} for $(a,b,c,e)=(A_i,A_{j-1},A_j,A_k)$ with $A_k\times A_{k+1}$ gives $\det(A_i,A_j,A_k)\det(A_k,A_{k+1},A_{j-1})=\det(A_{j-1},A_j,A_k)\det(A_k,A_{k+1},A_i)+\det(A_i,A_{j-1},A_k)\det(A_k,A_{k+1},A_j)$, whose right side is positive by induction on $j-i$ (when $k+1\equiv i$, the pole of $A_{k-1}A_k$ replaces that of $A_kA_{k+1}$).

The poles $n_0,\dots,n_{m-1}$ form a convex polygon, the polar polygon of $A$, whose corner at $n_i$ is $\pi-\ell_i$. The fan triangles $n_0n_an_{a+1}$, $1\le a\le m-2$, of the polar polygon have positive determinants, their angles add up to its corners, and the angles of a spherical triangle sum to more than $\pi$. So $\sum_i(\pi-\ell_i)>(m-2)\pi$, that is $\per A<2\pi$.

For the second claim, let $a,b\in\Sph$. The points $p$ of the open unit ball with $\langle p,a\rangle>0\ge\langle p,b\rangle$ form a wedge of the ball with dihedral angle $\dist(a,b)$, of volume $\frac23\dist(a,b)$. For a closed sequence $C_0,\dots,C_{l-1}$ of points of $\Sph$ and $p$ in the ball, let $c_C(p)$ be the number of indices $i$ at which the truth value of $\langle p,C_i\rangle>0$ differs from that of $\langle p,C_{i+1}\rangle>0$. Summing the volumes of the two wedges of each side gives $\int c_C=\frac43\per C$ over the ball. For the convex polygon $A$, $c_A(p)\le2$: four vertices $A_i,A_j,A_k,A_l$ with $i<j<k<l<i+m$, $\langle p,A_i\rangle>0$, $\langle p,A_j\rangle\le0$, $\langle p,A_k\rangle>0$ and $\langle p,A_l\rangle\le0$ would make the pairing of \eqref{eq:four} for $(a,b,c,e)=(A_i,A_j,A_k,A_l)$ with $p$ a sum of four nonnegative terms, two of them positive, since the four determinants are positive. Every point of the closed polygon of $A$ is a nonnegative combination of the three vertices $A_0,A_j,A_{j+1}$ of some fan triangle. So if $\langle p,A_i\rangle>0$ for every $i$, then $\langle p,y\rangle>0$ for every point $y$ of the closed polygon, and if $\langle p,A_i\rangle\le0$ for every $i$, then $\langle p,y\rangle\le0$ for all such $y$; in both cases $c_B(p)=0$. Otherwise $c_A(p)$, which is even, equals $2$, and $c_B(p)\le2$ as $B$ is convex. So $c_B\le c_A$ on the ball, and integrating gives $\per B\le\per A$. This is \lname{Tammes15.Geom.perim_mono} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:faces}]
The graph $G'(X)$ has no isolated vertex, and its edges are exposed pairs of its vertex set (proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:convex}). So Corollary~\ref{cor:twoconn} gives the connectivity, the 2-connectivity, the relation $n-E+F=2$ and face walks with at least three darts through distinct vertices, and Lemma~\ref{lem:convex} gives the convexity. The sides have length $d$ by the definition of $\CG(X)$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:polar} gives $md<2\pi$, hence $m<7$. A spherical triangle with three sides $d$ has three angles $\alpha(d)$ by the law of cosines, and the diagonal of a spherical rhombus with sides $d$ cuts it into two triangles with the same sides. In the formalization the lemma is the chain of Corollary~\ref{cor:twoconn} and Lemmas~\ref{lem:convex} and~\ref{lem:polar} for contact graphs, ending with \lname{Tammes15.FaceChain.faceSizes_of}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:threeconn}]
A vertex and three of its neighbours are four vertices, so it suffices to show that no two vertices $x,v$ separate $G$. Suppose they do. As $G-v$ is connected, every component of $G-\{x,v\}$ contains a neighbour of $x$; let $a$ and $b$ be neighbours of $x$ in different components. Call two neighbours of $x$ \emph{alike} if both differ from $v$ and lie in one component of $G-\{x,v\}$. For a dart $(x,w)$, let $w'$ be the end of $\rho(x,w)$; the face walk through the corner of $(x,w)$ runs through $w'$, $x$, $w$ in this order.

(i) If $w$ and $w'$ are not alike, the face walk through the corner of $(x,w)$ passes through $v$. Indeed its vertices are distinct, so its vertices from $w$ to $w'$ form a walk from $w$ to $w'$ in $G-x$.

(ii) If the face walks through the corners of two distinct darts $(x,w_1)$ and $(x,w_2)$ both pass through $v$, then $v\in\{w_1,w_1'\}$. A vertex $y\ne x,w_1,w_1'$ of the first walk has $\det(w_1',x,y)>0$ and $\det(x,w_1,y)>0$ by convexity, so its direction at $x$ lies strictly inside the corner of $(x,w_1)$. No neighbour of $x$ has its direction strictly inside a corner at $x$, by the definition of $\rho$ and since two neighbours in the same direction would coincide, the edges having one length; and the open corners at $x$ are disjoint. If $v\notin\{w_1,w_1'\}$, the direction of $v$ lies strictly inside the corner of $(x,w_1)$, so not strictly inside that of $(x,w_2)$, so $v\in\{w_2,w_2'\}$ is a neighbour of $x$ whose direction lies strictly inside the corner of $(x,w_1)$, which is impossible.

(iii) Going round $x$ in the order of $\rho$ from $a$ to $b$, and then from $b$ back to $a$, each of the two passages contains a dart $(x,w)$ such that $w$ and $w'$ are not alike. If $xv$ is not an edge, these are two distinct darts with $v\notin\{w,w'\}$, against (i) and (ii). If $xv$ is an edge, one of the passages avoids $v$, and its dart $(x,w)$ has $v\notin\{w,w'\}$; the face walk through the corner of $(x,v)$ passes through $v$, and (i) and (ii) applied to the darts $(x,w)$ and $(x,v)$ give a contradiction again. This is \lname{Tammes15.FaceChain.threeconn} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Rattlers}
The proofs of Propositions~\ref{prop:nor} and~\ref{prop:onehex} place a rattler at distance at least $h(d)$ from every side of its face, by the next two lemmas.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:edge}\leanmap{Tammes15.edge_distance, Tammes15.sin_sub_add_sin, Tammes15.hrad_bounds}
Let $0<d<\pi/2$ and $\dist(v,w)=d$. Then $d/2<h(d)<d$, and every point $p$ with $\dist(p,v)\ge d$ and $\dist(p,w)\ge d$ is at distance at least $h(d)$ from the arc $[vw]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The inequalities $\cos d<\cos^2(d/2)$ and $\cos d/\cos(d/2)>\cos d$ give $d/2<h(d)<d$. The point of $[vw]$ at distance $t\in[0,d]$ from $v$ is $x(t)=(\sin(d-t)\,v+\sin t\,w)/\sin d$, with nonnegative coefficients. As $\langle p,v\rangle\le\cos d$ and $\langle p,w\rangle\le\cos d$,
\[
\langle p,x(t)\rangle\le\cos d\,\frac{\sin(d-t)+\sin t}{\sin d}=\cos d\,\frac{\cos(d/2-t)}{\cos(d/2)}\le\frac{\cos d}{\cos(d/2)}=\cos h(d),
\]
so $\dist(p,x(t))\ge h(d)$. This is \lname{Tammes15.edge_distance} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:disc}\leanmap{Tammes15.Geom.disc_side, Tammes15.Geom.inClosed_of_disc}\mapnote{for faces in cone form, by bounding the distance from $r$ to the great circle of each side}
Let $0<d<\pi/2$, and let $A$ be a convex polygon with all sides of length $d$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $r$ lies inside $A$ and $\dist(r,A_i)\ge d$ for every $i$, then $\det(A_i,A_{i+1},r)\ge\sin d\sin h(d)$ for every $i$.
\item If $c\in\Sph$ has $\det(A_i,A_{i+1},c)\ge\sin d\sin h(d)$ for every $i$, then the closed disc $D(c,h(d))$ of centre $c$ and radius $h(d)$ lies in the closed polygon of $A$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

As $|A_i\times A_{i+1}|=\sin d$, the inequality of (1) says that $r$ lies on the left of the great circle of every side, at distance at least $h(d)$ from it.

\begin{proof}
Let $n_i=A_i\times A_{i+1}/\sin d$ be the unit pole of the side $A_iA_{i+1}$ and $h=h(d)$. For (1), let $\sigma_i=\langle n_i,r\rangle>0$, and choose $i$ with $\sigma=\sigma_i$ minimal. If $\sigma=1$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise $p=r-\sigma n_i$ has $\langle p,n_i\rangle=0$ and $|p|^2=\langle r,p\rangle=1-\sigma^2>0$, and $\langle p,n_j\rangle=\sigma_j-\sigma\langle n_i,n_j\rangle\ge\sigma_j-\sigma\ge0$ for every $j$. As $p$ is orthogonal to $A_i\times A_{i+1}$, $p=\lambda A_i+\mu A_{i+1}$, and $\langle p,A_{i+1}\times A_{i+2}\rangle=\lambda\det(A_{i+1},A_{i+2},A_i)\ge0$ and $\langle p,A_{i-1}\times A_i\rangle=\mu\det(A_{i-1},A_i,A_{i+1})\ge0$ give $\lambda,\mu\ge0$. So $p/|p|$ is a point of the side $[A_iA_{i+1}]$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:edge} gives $\langle r,p/|p|\rangle\le\cos h$, that is $\sqrt{1-\sigma^2}\le\cos h$. Hence $\sigma\ge\sin h$, and $\det(A_j,A_{j+1},r)=\sin d\,\sigma_j\ge\sin d\sin h$ for every $j$.

For (2), $\langle n_i,c\rangle\ge\sin h$, so the component of $n_i$ orthogonal to $c$ has norm at most $\cos h$. A point of $D(c,h)$ is $y=\cos t\,c+\sin t\,e$ with $0\le t\le h$ and $e$ a unit vector orthogonal to $c$, and $\langle n_i,y\rangle\ge\cos t\sin h-\sin t\cos h=\sin(h-t)\ge0$. Item (1) is \lname{Tammes15.Geom.disc_side} in the formalization, and item (2) a lemma on discs there.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:onehex} compares the perimeter of a hexagon with that of a polygon built from two discs of radius $h(d)$ at distance $d$ (Figure~\ref{fig:polygon}), whose sides are given by the following functions. For $0\le h<\pi/2$ and $0\le s\le\pi$ put $\operatorname{ch}_h(s)=\arccos(\cos^2h+\sin^2h\cos s)$, the distance between two points of a circle of radius $h$ whose directions from the centre make the angle $s$. For $0\le l<\pi$ put
\[
\ell_t(l,h)=\arccos\frac{\cos l-\sin^2h}{\cos^2h},\qquad\gamma_t(l,h)=\arccos\Bigl(-\tan h\tan\frac l2\Bigr),
\]
the first for $l\le\pi-2h$ and the second for $\tan h\tan(l/2)\le1$, and $P_{10}(l,h)=2\ell_t(l,h)+8\operatorname{ch}_h\bigl((2\pi-2\gamma_t(l,h))/4\bigr)$.

\begin{proof}
For (1), $\cos \ell_t=1-(1-\cos l)/\cos^2h$ decreases in $l\in[0,\pi]$ and in $h\in[0,\pi/2)$; $-\tan h\tan\frac l2$ decreases in $h\in[0,\pi/2)$ and in $l\in[0,\pi)$; and $\cos^2h+\sin^2h\cos s=1-(1-\cos s)\sin^2h$ decreases in $h\in[0,\pi/2]$ and in $s\in[0,\pi]$. Since $\arccos$ decreases, $\ell_t$, $\gamma_t$ and $\operatorname{ch}_h(s)$ increase as stated.

For (2), note first that $\pi-2h(d)-d>0$ (Section~\ref{sec:constants}), so that $\ell_t(d,h)$ is defined, $\tan h\tan\frac d2<1$ since $d<\pi/3$, so that $\gamma_t$ is defined, and $T'$, $F'$ complete $C'$ to an orthonormal basis, $T'$ being the direction at $C'$ away from $C$, as $T$ is the direction at $C$ away from $C'$. Two great circles are tangent to both circles with both discs on one side. Place one of them on the equator, with points of tangency $t_{1,2}=(\cos\frac{\ell_t}2,\mp\sin\frac{\ell_t}2,0)$ and centres $r_{1,2}=(\cos h\cos\frac{\ell_t}2,\mp\cos h\sin\frac{\ell_t}2,\sin h)$. Then $\cos d=\langle r_1,r_2\rangle=\cos^2h\cos\ell_t+\sin^2h$, which is the formula for $\ell_t=\ell_t(d,h)$, the length of the arc $t_1t_2$. The angle at $r_1$ between the directions to $t_1$ and to $r_2$ has cosine $(\langle t_1,r_2\rangle-\cos h\cos d)/(\sin h\sin d)=\cos h(\cos\ell_t-\cos d)/(\sin h\sin d)=-\tan h\tan\frac d2$, so it is $\gamma_t$. The points $q_0$ and $q_4$ make the angle $2s=\pi-\gamma_t$ with $T$, hence the angle $\gamma_t$ with the direction from $C$ to $C'$, and so do $q_5$ and $q_9$ about $C'$; they are the points of tangency, and $q_4$ and $q_5$ lie on the same side of the great circle through $C$ and $C'$, as do $q_9$ and $q_0$. So the sides $q_4q_5$ and $q_9q_0$ have length $\ell_t(d,h)$, and the eight other sides are chords subtending the angle $s$ at a centre, of length $\operatorname{ch}_h(s)$, which gives the perimeter.

It remains to show that $q_0,\dots,q_9$ is a convex polygon. Let $S$ be the rotation by $\pi$ about the unit vector along $C+C'$. In the basis $(T,F,C)$,
\[
S(v_1T+v_2F+v_3C)=(-\cos d\,v_1-\sin d\,v_3)\,T-v_2\,F+(-\sin d\,v_1+\cos d\,v_3)\,C ,
\]
so $S$ maps $C$, $T$, $F$ to $C'$, $T'$, $F'$, and as $S^2=1$, $Sq_j=q_{j+5}$ with indices modulo $10$. As $S$ preserves determinants, it suffices to prove $\det(q_i,q_{i+1},q_k)>0$ for $i=0,\dots,4$ and every $k\not\equiv i,i+1$. Put $m(t)=\cos h\,C+\sin h(\cos t\,T+\sin t\,F)$, so that $q_j=m(t_j)$ and $q_{j+5}=Sm(t_j)$ for $j=0,\dots,4$. Since $\tan h\tan\frac d2\in(0,1)$, $\gamma_t\in(\pi/2,\pi)$ and $0<s<\pi/4$. All of $\sin h$, $\cos h$, $\sin d$, $\cos d$ are positive.

\emph{A chord against its own circle.} For $i\le3$ let $\mu=(t_i+t_{i+1})/2$. A direct computation gives
\[
q_i\times q_{i+1}=2\sin h\sin\tfrac s2\;n_\mu,\qquad n_\mu=-\cos h\cos\mu\,T-\cos h\sin\mu\,F+\sin h\cos\tfrac s2\,C,
\]
and $\langle n_\mu,m(t)\rangle=\sin h\cos h\bigl(\cos\frac s2-\cos(t-\mu)\bigr)$. For $k\le4$ with $k\ne i,i+1$, $|t_k-\mu|=|k-i-\frac12|\,s$ lies in $[\frac32s,\frac72s]$, inside $(\frac s2,\pi)$, so $\det(q_i,q_{i+1},q_k)>0$.

\emph{The same chord against the other circle.} For $k\le4$, $\langle n_\mu,Sm(t_k)\rangle=\langle Sn_\mu,m(t_k)\rangle$, and
\begin{gather*}
Sn_\mu=a\,T+b\,F+X\,C,\qquad b=\cos h\sin\mu,\\
a=\cos h\cos\mu\cos d-\sin h\cos\tfrac s2\sin d,\qquad X=\cos h\cos\mu\sin d+\sin h\cos\tfrac s2\cos d .
\end{gather*}
So $\langle Sn_\mu,m(t)\rangle=\sin h(a\cos t+b\sin t)+X\cos h\ge X\cos h-\sin h\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, and $a^2+b^2+X^2=|n_\mu|^2=\cos^2h+\sin^2h\cos^2\frac s2$. If $X>0$, the last bound is positive exactly when $X>\sin h\sqrt{\cos^2h+\sin^2h\cos^2(s/2)}$. As $|\mu|\le\frac32s<\frac\pi2$, $X\ge\cos h\sin d\cos\frac{3s}2+\sin h\cos\frac s2\cos d$, and it suffices that
\[
\sin h\sqrt{\cos^2h+\sin^2h\cos^2\tfrac s2}<\cos h\sin d\cos\tfrac{3s}2+\sin h\cos\tfrac s2\cos d ,
\]
an inequality in $d$ alone, with $h=h(d)$ and $s=(\pi-\gamma_t(d,h))/2$. It holds on $[\dlo,\dhi]$ (Section~\ref{sec:constants}): Lean proves it by bounding each factor by its value at an end of the interval, through rational enclosures, and the bound so obtained still holds, by $0.0037$ in the square roots of the two sides.

\emph{The tangent side.} Let $\hat n=\cos h\cos\gamma_t\,T-\cos h\sin\gamma_t\,F+\sin h\,C$, a unit vector. The relation $\cos\gamma_t=-\tan h\tan\frac d2$ gives $\cos h\cos\gamma_t(1+\cos d)=-\sin h\sin d$, and with it $S\hat n=\cos h\cos\gamma_t\,T+\cos h\sin\gamma_t\,F+\sin h\,C$. Hence
\begin{gather*}
\langle\hat n,m(t)\rangle=\sin h\cos h\bigl(1-\cos(t-t_4)\bigr),\\
\langle\hat n,Sm(t)\rangle=\langle S\hat n,m(t)\rangle=\sin h\cos h\bigl(1-\cos(t-t_0)\bigr),
\end{gather*}
as $t_4=2s=\pi-\gamma_t$ and $t_0=-2s$. So $\hat n$ is orthogonal to $q_4$ and $q_5=Sm(t_0)$, and $q_4\times q_5=\lambda\hat n$, where a computation of the last coordinate gives $\lambda\cos h=\sin\gamma_t\sin d>0$. For $k\ne4,5$, $\det(q_4,q_5,q_k)=\lambda\langle\hat n,q_k\rangle>0$, since $t_k-t_4\in[-4s,-s]$ for $k\le3$ and $t_{k-5}-t_0\in[s,4s]$ for $k\ge6$. This proves the convexity. The polygon of the formalization is \lname{Tammes15.Geom.qpoly}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:nor}]
Let $r$ be such a point, $A=A_0,\dots,A_{m-1}$ with $m\le5$, and $\varepsilon_i$ the angle at $r$ between the directions to $A_i$ and $A_{i+1}$. As $\det(r,A_i,A_{i+1})>0$, the direction to $A_{i+1}$ follows the direction to $A_i$ counterclockwise about $r$ by $\varepsilon_i\in(0,\pi)$; let $\theta_0<\theta_1<\dots<\theta_m$ be the lifted angles, with $\theta_{i+1}-\theta_i=\varepsilon_i$, so that $\theta_m-\theta_0=\sum_i\varepsilon_i$ is a positive multiple of $2\pi$. The pairing of \eqref{eq:four} for $(a,b,c,e)=(A_0,A_1,A_j,A_l)$ with $A_0\times r$ gives
\[
\det(A_0,A_j,r)\det(A_0,A_1,A_l)=\det(A_0,A_l,r)\det(A_0,A_1,A_j)+\det(A_0,A_j,A_l)\det(A_0,A_1,r),
\]
where, for $1<j<l<m$, the three determinants of vertices of $A$ are positive (proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:polar}), and so is $\det(A_0,A_1,r)$. So $\det(A_0,A_j,r)\le0$ implies $\det(A_0,A_l,r)<0$. As $\det(A_0,A_1,r)>0>\det(A_0,A_{m-1},r)$, the sign of $\sin(\theta_j-\theta_0)$, which is that of $\det(A_0,A_j,r)$, changes once along $j=1,\dots,m-1$, and with steps below $\pi$ this gives $\theta_{m-1}-\theta_0<2\pi$, so $\sum_i\varepsilon_i<3\pi$ and $\sum_i\varepsilon_i=2\pi$.

Write $c=\cos d$, $h=h(d)$, $p=\langle r,A_i\rangle$ and $q=\langle r,A_{i+1}\rangle$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:disc}(1), $\langle r,A_i\times A_{i+1}\rangle\ge\sin d\sin h$. The Gram determinant of $A_i,A_{i+1},r$ is $\langle r,A_i\times A_{i+1}\rangle^2=1-c^2-p^2-q^2+2cpq$, and $\cos h=c/\cos(d/2)$ gives $\cos^2h=2c^2/(1+c)$, so
\[
p^2+q^2-2cpq\le(1-c^2)\cos^2h=2c^2(1-c).
\]
With $2pq\le p^2+q^2$ this gives $pq\le c^2$. The law of cosines at $r$, the inequality $(1-p^2)(1-q^2)\le(1-pq)^2$ and $c-pq\ge0$ give
\[
\cos\varepsilon_i=\frac{c-pq}{\sqrt{(1-p^2)(1-q^2)}}\ge\frac{c-pq}{1-pq}\ge\frac{c-c^2}{1-c^2}=\cos\alpha(d),
\]
since $(c-t)/(1-t)$ decreases in $t<1$. So $\varepsilon_i\le\alpha(d)\le\alpha(\dhi)=69.23^\circ<72^\circ$ for each of the at most five sides, and the $\varepsilon_i$ cannot sum to $2\pi$. This is \lname{Tammes15.Geom.nor} in the formalization.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:onehex}]
Suppose there are, and put $l=\dist(r_1,r_2)$ and $h=h(d)$. Then $l<\pi$, since $r_2=-r_1$ would give $\det(A_0,A_1,r_2)<0$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:disc}(1), $\det(A_i,A_{i+1},r_k)\ge\sin d\sin h$ for $k=1,2$ and every $i$. Let $c$ be the point of $[r_1r_2]$ with $\dist(r_1,c)=d$. From $c=(\sin(l-d)r_1+\sin d\,r_2)/\sin l$,
\[
\det(A_i,A_{i+1},c)\ge\sin d\sin h\,\frac{\sin(l-d)+\sin d}{\sin l}=\sin d\sin h\,\frac{\cos(l/2-d)}{\cos(l/2)}\ge\sin d\sin h .
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:disc}(2), $D(r_1,h)$ and $D(c,h)$ lie in the closed polygon of $A$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:perims}(2) with $C=r_1$, $T=(\cos d\,r_1-c)/\sin d$ and $F=C\times T$, so that $C'=c$: the ten vertices of its polygon lie in the closed polygon of $A$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:polar} gives $P_{10}(d,h(d))\le\per A=6d$. Cut $[\dlo,\dhi]$ into ten equal intervals $[a',b']$, of length $0.301375^\circ$. For $d\in[a',b']$, Lemma~\ref{lem:perims}(1) and the monotonicity of $h$ give
\[
P_{10}(d,h(d))-6d\ge2\ell_t(a',h(a'))+8\operatorname{ch}_{h(a')}\bigl((2\pi-2\gamma_t(b',h(b')))/4\bigr)-6b',
\]
and the right side is at least $2.48^\circ$ on each of the ten intervals, a contradiction. This is \lname{Tammes15.Geom.onehex} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:k3} finds the face walk of a rattler by the next lemma.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:inside}\leanmap{Tammes15.Geom.exists_face_inside}
Let $G$ be a graph without isolated vertices on a nonempty finite set of points of $\Sph$, whose edges are minor arcs of one length $d\in(0,\pi/2)$, drawn with its rotation, with all its corners in $(0,\pi)$, and whose face walks have at least three darts and are convex polygons. Then every point of $\Sph$ at distance greater than $d$ from every vertex lies inside some face walk.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $y$ be such a point. The union of the arcs of the edges is compact; let $z$ be a point of it nearest to $y$. No point of an arc is at distance more than $d$ from both of its ends, so $y$ lies on no arc, and $z\ne-y$, since the arcs are not reduced to points. If $z$ were a vertex $v$, the corners at $v$ being below $\pi$, some neighbour $w$ of $v$ would make an acute angle at $v$ with the direction of $y$, and moving from $v$ along the arc $vw$ would bring the point nearer to $y$. So $z$ lies inside the arc of an edge $ab$, and the minor arc from $z$ to $y$ meets this arc orthogonally at $z$. Orient the edge so that $\det(a,b,y)>0$, and let $W$ be the face walk of the dart $(a,b)$. Near $z$, the arc from $z$ to $y$ lies inside $W$, since $z$ lies inside the side $ab$ and every other side has $z$ strictly on its left. If $y$ were not inside $W$, the first point of the arc from $z$ to $y$ that is not inside $W$ would be a point of the closed polygon at which $\det(A_j,A_{j+1},\cdot)$ vanishes for some side, hence a point of that side (proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:disc}), on an arc and nearer to $y$ than $z$. This is \lname{Tammes15.Geom.exists_face_inside} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:k3}]
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:minimal} and~\ref{lem:faces}, $G'(X_0)$ satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:inside}, with face walks that are convex polygons with three to six sides of length $d$. A rattler is at distance greater than $d$ from every other point of $X_0$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:inside} it lies inside a face walk $W$, which has six darts by Proposition~\ref{prop:nor}; by Proposition~\ref{prop:onehex}, no other rattler lies inside $W$. So $G'(X_0)$ has at least $k$ face walks with six darts. With $n=15-k$ vertices, $E$ edges and $F$ face walks, $2E\ge3F+3k$, $F=E-n+2$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:faces}) and $2E\ge3n$ give $3n/2\le E\le3n-6-3k$, so $k\le n/2-2$, that is $3k\le11$. The count is \lname{Tammes15.k_le_three_of_hex} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:regions}}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:regions}]
Item (1) is the remark at the start of the section, together with the fact that a point of the arc $ab$ is within $d/2$ of $a$ or $b$. For a face walk $W$, let $P_W$ be its inside and $\bar P_W$ its closed polygon. The set $P_W$ is open and contains the normalized sum of the vertices of $W$; for $z,z'\in P_W$, $z'\ne-z$ and the minor arc from $z$ to $z'$ stays in $P_W$, so $P_W$ is connected; and its closure is $\bar P_W$. A point of $\bar P_W$ on the great circle of a side lies on that side (proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:disc}), so the boundary of $P_W$ is the union of the sides of $W$.

\emph{No vertex outside $W$ lies in $\bar P_W$, and no point of an arc lies in $P_W$.} Let $v$ be a vertex of $W$, with neighbours $v^-$ before it and $v^+$ after it on $W$. A neighbour $y\ne v^\pm$ of $v$ does not satisfy both $\det(v^-,v,y)>0$ and $\det(v,v^+,y)>0$, which would put its direction at $v$ strictly inside a corner at $v$ (proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:threeconn}). Let $U$ be the set of vertices not on $W$ that lie in $\bar P_W$; by (1) and the boundary of $P_W$ they lie in $P_W$. Let $x\in U$ and $x'$ a neighbour of $x$. If $x'$ were on $W$, then $x\in P_W$ would be a neighbour of $x'$ excluded above. If $x'$ were outside $\bar P_W$, the arc $xx'$, which is connected and meets $P_W$ and its exterior, would meet a side of $W$, at a common vertex by (1), that is at $x$ or $x'$, neither of which is on $W$. So $x'\in U$. As $G'(X)$ is connected and $W$ has vertices outside $U$, $U$ is empty. Now let $ab$ be an edge that is not a side of $W$. If $a$ is on $W$, then for one of the two sides $g$ of $W$ at $a$, $\det(g,b)\le0$, where $\det(g,\cdot)$ is the determinant with the two ends of $g$; as $\det(g,a)=0$, every point of the arc, a nonnegative combination of $a$ and $b$, has $\det(g,\cdot)\le0$ and is not in $P_W$. If neither end is on $W$, both lie outside $\bar P_W$, and an arc meeting $P_W$ would meet a side of $W$ at a common vertex, on $W$. The points of the sides of $W$ are not in $P_W$.

\emph{Every point of $\Sph$ lies in some $\bar P_W$.} Let $z\in\Sph$ and $a$ a vertex nearest to $z$; we may assume $z\ne a$, and then $z\ne-a$, as $a$ has a neighbour, nearer to $-a$ than $a$. The direction of $z$ at $a$ lies in the closed corner of some dart $(a,w)$; let $v_0,\dots,v_{m-1}$ be the face walk through this corner, with $v_0=a$, $v_1=w$ and $v_{m-1}$ the end of $\rho(a,w)$. Then $\det(v_0,v_1,z)\ge0\ge\det(v_0,v_{m-1},z)$, so some $i$ with $1\le i\le m-2$ has $\det(v_0,v_i,z)\ge0\ge\det(v_0,v_{i+1},z)$. As $\det(v_0,v_i,v_{i+1})>0$ (proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:polar}), write $z=\lambda v_0+\mu v_i+\nu v_{i+1}$; Cramer's rule gives $\mu\ge0$ and $\nu\ge0$. Since $a$ is nearest to $z$,
\[
0\le\langle v_0-v_i,z\rangle=(\lambda-\mu)(1-\langle v_0,v_i\rangle)+\nu(\langle v_0,v_{i+1}\rangle-\cos d),
\]
and $\langle v_0,v_{i+1}\rangle\le\cos d$, so $\lambda\ge\mu\ge0$. So $z$ is a nonnegative combination of vertices of $W$, and lies in $\bar P_W$.

\emph{Conclusion.} By the two previous paragraphs, $P_W$ is connected, misses the drawing, and is closed in its complement, since its boundary lies in the drawing; so $P_W$ is a component of the complement. A point off the drawing lies in some $\bar P_W$ and not on its boundary, hence in $P_W$; so every component is some $P_W$. If $P_W=P_{W'}$, the boundaries agree, so by (1) every side of $W'$ is a side of $W$, traversed in the same direction since $P_W$ lies on the left of both; a face walk is determined by one of its darts, so $W'=W$. The arc of an edge lies in $\bar P_W$ only if it is a side of $W$, by the second paragraph, and the $m$ sides of $W$ are distinct edges, as its $m\ge3$ vertices are distinct. The last sentence follows by Theorem~\ref{thm:structure}(3), since $G'(X_0)$ is 3-connected, hence connected, and has all its corners in $[\alpha(d),\pi)$. This is \lname{Tammes15.D2Regions.regions_eq_facePolygons} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\section{From the programs to D2 and D3}\label{app:programs}

This appendix proves Proposition~\ref{prop:lists}: the list $\mathcal L$ satisfies D2, and D3 with $\{\CI,\CIII\}$. It first describes the arithmetic of the program and the formal model of its procedures, and proves them sound (Section~\ref{sec:model}); then it derives D2 from the description of plantri (Section~\ref{sec:dtwoproof}) and carries the verdicts of the program to D3 (Section~\ref{sec:dthreeproof}). \subsection{The arithmetic and the formal model of the program}\label{sec:model}
All arithmetic is on intervals of IEEE~754 binary64 numbers. Each of $+,-,\times,/,\sqrt{\ }$ is computed with rounding to nearest and widened by one unit in the last place on each side, which contains the exact result. The elementary functions are computed from these operations only: for $\cos,\sin$ the argument is reduced by $\pi/2$ split into three parts, two of them with at most $32$ significant bits, so that the products by the quotient are exact for $|k|<2^{21}$, and the Taylor series of degree $20$ and $21$ carry the Lagrange remainders $|x|^{22}/22!$ and $|x|^{23}/23!$; $\arctan$ uses $\arctan t=\arctan c+\arctan((t-c)/(1+tc))$ with $c$ the nearest multiple of $1/8$, enclosed values of $\arctan(j/8)$ and the terms up to $u^{13}/13$ of the alternating series with remainder $|u|^{15}/15$; $\arccos$ and $\arcsin$ are expressed through $\arctan$ and square roots. The constants are enclosures computed in PARI/GP with 200 digits; $\pi_-<\pi<\pi_+$ denote the enclosing binary64 numbers of $\pi$.

The program has two guards. The triangle angle of (T2) evaluates the arc cosine at ends of its three arguments chosen by the signs of the derivatives, which is valid when the three sides lie in $(0,\pi)$; its arguments are computed enclosures, such as the base of (T1), which lie in $(0,\pi)$ only when they are tight. The first guard returns $[0,\pi_+]$ unless the three arguments lie in $(0,\pi_-)$. The side of (T9) evaluates the cosine of the upper end of the angle, clamped to $\pi_+$, while $\cos g$ is monotone in the angle on $[0,\pi]$ only; the second guard reads that cosine as $-1$ when the upper end is at least $\pi_-$.

The program builds its procedures from a few operations of interval arithmetic, described in the following terms. A binary64 number is read as an extended real number, the two zeros being one value, or as NaN, and compared as in IEEE~754, so that every comparison with NaN is false. An interval is a pair of such numbers, its ends, and it contains a real number $t$ if its lower end is at most $t$ and $t$ is at most its upper end.

For a graph and a set of $k$ of its hexagons, the program builds the case of one choice of these hexagons, its \emph{choice} for that set; at the first level it computes the narrowing from the set alone. The \emph{procedures of the program} are as follows. On a box of a case that it builds whose ends are binary64 numbers, the narrowings are those of the two levels of the program; on every other box, and on every box of a case that it does not build, the narrowing keeps the box. The enclosure, which depends on the graph and not on the choice of hexagons, is the triangle angle of the program on a box whose ends are binary64 numbers, and gives no interval on any other box. Their soundness is stated over an arbitrary arithmetic, as follows.

On a box in the domain with binary64 ends, of a case that the program builds, the narrowing ends a run by (6); on every other box, and on every box of a case that the program does not build, the narrowing keeps the box, which is the end of the run that stops at once. The program evaluates the relations of (T5) and (T6) at ends of the box chosen by monotonicity, as follows.
The maps of (T5) and (T6) are monotone in each corner under conditions that are tested on the box. Write an output as $C(\theta)=\beta(\theta)+\Gamma(e(\theta))$, where $e(\theta)$ and $\beta(\theta)$ are the base and the base angle of the isosceles triangle with apex $\theta$ (T1), $\Gamma$ is the angle of the middle triangle between the side $e$ and a second side, and $X$ is the angle of the middle triangle opposite that second side. In a triangle with sides $a,b,c$ and angle $\Gamma$ between $a$ and $b$, the derivative of $\Gamma$ in $a$ at fixed $b$ and $c$ is $-\cot B/\sin a$, where $B$ is the angle opposite $b$. With $\beta'(\theta)=-\cos d/(2\cos^2(e/2))$ and $e'(\theta)=\sin d\cos(\theta/2)/\cos(e/2)$ this gives
\[
C'(\theta)=-\frac{\sin d\,\bigl(\cos d\sin\frac\theta2+\cot X\cos\frac\theta2\bigr)}{2\sin\frac e2\cos^2\frac e2},\qquad\cos d\sin\frac\theta2+\cot X\cos\frac\theta2=\frac{\cos\frac\theta2\,\sin(\beta+X)}{\sin\beta\,\sin X},
\]
the identity by Napier's rule $\cos d=\cot(\theta/2)\cot\beta$. So $C$ is non-increasing in $\theta$ where $\theta\le\pi$ and $\beta+X\le\pi$. An output is non-decreasing, with no condition, in the corner whose base is the side opposite the output angle, by (T2), since $e$ is increasing on $(0,\pi]$; and $e(2\pi-\theta)=e(\theta)$. Where no triangle exists the clamped arc cosine is constant, so the monotonicity survives the clamp. Where the test succeeds, the values at the corners of the box are intersected with the natural interval extension.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:sound}\leanmap{Tammes15.Contractors.Impl.onDom_sound, Tammes15.Contractors.progTrees_onDom, Tammes15.Contractors.primStep_sound, Tammes15.Contractors.primStep_subset, Tammes15.Contractors.Run.sound, Tammes15.Contractors.Run.subset, Tammes15.Contractors.wheelOut_sound}\mapnote{for all cases at once, with the procedures as a parameter of the statement and the variable of each corner attached to its dart, the first declaration, and for the trees of every case of a list, the second}

\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let an assignment with $\dlo\le d\le\dhi$ satisfy the relation system, and let a box $B$ in the domain contain its values. A primitive step computed with a sound arithmetic sends $B$ to a box that contains them. Each contractor is a consequence of the relation system: the rows of the first level as shown in (2) below, (T5) to (T8), with the ranges of their arc cosines, are relations of the system, (T3) is the definition of $\alpha$, (T4) follows from $y=\operatorname{rh}_d(x)$ for corners below $\pi$, and (T9) follows from (T8) since $\sin r_i\sin d>0$; the triangle angle is the evaluation of (T2) over the box. The two guards make the triangle angle and the side valid for every sound arithmetic, without the tight enclosures that the evaluation at ends needs otherwise. The domain keeps $d$ in $(0,\pi/2)$ and the variables positive; at the upper end of a corner above $\pi$, the bound (T7) is at most $\pi/3<\alpha(d)$, which uses $d\le1$ and the bound $3.2$ of the corner. A step never enlarges a box. By induction on the run, a run from $B$ ends at a box that contains the values, and never at empty: a cut removes a part from which a run ends at empty, and which therefore holds no values of such an assignment. So each narrowing of $N'$ keeps the values, also off the domain, where it keeps the box. The triangle angle of the program contains $\gamma(r_{i-1};r_i,d)$ by (T2), and so does every interval that contains it. Hence $N'$ is sound. Runs only shrink boxes and the halves of a split lie in the box, so every box that the acceptance of a tree from a box in the domain reads lies in the domain, where $N'$ agrees with $N$. The formal proof treats every primitive step, NaN and infinite ends included, for every sound arithmetic. This is \lname{Tammes15.Contractors.Impl.onDom_sound} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\subsection{D2}\label{sec:dtwoproof}
Let $\mathcal L$ be the list of plane graphs of Computation~\ref{comp:enum}, each with the rotation that takes a dart at a vertex to the next one counterclockwise, which is the previous neighbour in the clockwise order of the planar code of plantri.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dtwo}\mapnote{not formalized; D2 is a hypothesis of Theorems \ref{thm:formal} and \ref{thm:trees}}\codemap{code/impl1/stageA_check.sh > code/impl1/out/stageA_parts.txt}
The list $\mathcal L$ satisfies D2.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the assumption of Section~\ref{sec:notlean}, plantri meets its description \cite{BM}, which concerns graphs drawn on the sphere without crossings. Their faces are the regions into which the drawing divides the sphere, and the size of a face is the number of edges that bound it. A drawing is represented by the clockwise order of the edges at each vertex, seen from outside, and an isomorphism of two drawings is a bijection of their vertices and of their edges that carries the clockwise order at every vertex to the clockwise order at its image, or at every vertex to the reversed order. A graph is 3-connected if it has at least four vertices and removing at most two of them leaves it connected. For the options of Computation~\ref{comp:enum}, the description states that the output contains exactly one member of each isomorphism class of 3-connected drawings on $n$ vertices with faces of size at most $6$. For $n=14$ and $15$ the list is the union of the outputs of the parts of Computation~\ref{comp:enum}; the description states that the parameter res/mod divides the output into mod disjoint classes and that the run with res produces the class res, so this union is the output of the run without splitting. The output filter is a procedure that plantri calls on each graph it would write, and by the same assumption it keeps the graph exactly when every vertex has degree at most $5$.

Let $P$ be a plane graph as in Definition~\ref{def:dtwo}, with $n$ vertices and rotation $\rho$, and let $x$ be a drawing of its graph with rotation $\rho$ and all regions of size at most $6$. The points and arcs of $x$ form a 3-connected graph drawn on the sphere without crossings, with faces of size at most $6$, and its clockwise order at each vertex is $\rho^{-1}$, since $\rho$ takes each dart to the next one counterclockwise. So the output of plantri contains a drawing isomorphic to it: an isomorphism of the graphs that carries $\rho^{-1}$ to the clockwise orders $\sigma$ of the output graph or to $\sigma^{-1}$. The rotation of that graph in $\mathcal L$ is $\sigma^{-1}$, so the isomorphism carries $\rho$ to it or to its inverse, and $P$ is isomorphic up to reflection to that graph. It has the degrees of $P$, at most $5$, so the filter keeps it, and it is in $\mathcal L$.
\end{proof}

A set $\mathcal P$ of plane graphs satisfies the \emph{verdicts of the program} if for every $P\in \mathcal P$ with at most fifteen vertices and every choice $H$ of hexagons of $P$ there is a choice $H_0$ of hexagons in the same faces such that every assignment with $\dlo\le d\le\dhi$ that satisfies the relation system for $H_0$ has a gluing for which Pair fires on $Y'$ or Local fires pointwise on $Y'$, in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:soundtrees}.
\end{definition}


The verdicts of the program are the form in which the search reports its refutations; the next lemma turns them into D3.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:verdicts}\leanmap{Tammes15.PaperSteps.killed_of_progKilled, Tammes15.PaperSteps.exists_shift_data, Tammes15.PaperSteps.relSys_relabel, Tammes15.PaperSteps.glueY_relabel, Tammes15.PaperSteps.pairFires_of_equiv, Tammes15.PaperSteps.localFires_of_equiv, Tammes15.PaperSteps.glueY_theta, Tammes15.PaperSteps.frameN_theta, Tammes15.PaperSteps.free_angle_sum, Tammes15.PaperSteps.localFires_of_tieFires, Tammes15.PaperSteps.inTieBoxes_pt, Tammes15.PaperSteps.tie_idx, Tammes15.PaperSteps.normalize_isometry}
If a set $\mathcal P$ of plane graphs satisfies the verdicts of the program, then it satisfies D3 with $\{\CI,\CIII\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $P\in \mathcal P$, let $H$ be a choice of hexagons and $H_0$ the choice that the verdicts give, and take an assignment with $d\in[\dlo,\dhi]$ that satisfies the relation system for $H$. Three steps carry the verdicts to the gluing of Definition~\ref{def:glue}.

(i) The choices $H$ and $H_0$ differ by a relabelling of the free points and a cyclic shift of the corners of each hexagon. Under this change an assignment that satisfies the relation system for one choice gives one that satisfies it for the other, since the wheel relations from another base dart are the wheel relations with the indices shifted; a gluing gives a gluing that places each free point from the same vertex, and the glued configurations have the same points, up to the relabelling. Pair and Local do not see the labels of the points, so they fire on both configurations or on neither. So the verdicts give, for the assignment relabelled for $H_0$, a gluing for which Pair or Local fires on $Y'$.

(ii) Let $\Theta=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1)$. Take the gluing of Definition~\ref{def:glue} with the root, the reference dart, the tree and the corners of the free points of that gluing. Both constructions multiply the factors $R_z(\phi)R_y(d)Z$ along the tree, with the sums $\phi$ of corners taken in opposite rotation orders; as the corners at a vertex sum to $2\pi$, each turn $\tau$ of Definition~\ref{def:glue} is $2\pi$ minus the turn $\tau'$ of the program. Since $\Theta R_z(\phi)\Theta=R_z(-\phi)$, $\Theta R_y(d)\Theta=R_y(d)$, $\Theta Z\Theta=Z$ and $\Theta e_3=e_3$, the frames of Definition~\ref{def:glue} are the frames $F'$ conjugated by $\Theta$. A free point is placed from $A_i$ through $A_{i+1}$ in Definition~\ref{def:glue} and through $A_{i-1}$ by the program, and the angle of Definition~\ref{def:glue} is again $2\pi$ minus that of the program, by the corner equation $\theta_i=\gamma(r_{i+1};r_i,d)+\gamma(r_{i-1};r_i,d)$ of (T8). So the glued configuration is $Y=\Theta Y'$, and Pair fires on $Y$ if it fires on $Y'$.

(iii) Suppose that Local fires pointwise on $Y'$. The boxes of the data contain the exact frame points: with $u$ and $b$ enclosed in intervals of widths $10^{-60}$ and $3\cdot10^{-48}$, each coordinate of a frame point is within $10^{-39}$ of the midpoint of its interval, whose radius is $10^{-38}$. Each configuration of the data lists, in some order, the points of the image of $\CI$ or $\CIII$ under an element of $\Delta$, and its thirty contacts are those of that image. Take for $z$ the exact frame points. Then $z_a$ and $z_b$ are unit vectors with $\langle z_a,z_b\rangle=u$, so the map that gives the target is orthogonal, and $|Y'(v)-O'q_{j'(v)}|\le1.04\cdot10^{-3}$ for every point $v$, with $q=\CI$ or $\CIII$, an orthogonal map $O'$ and a bijection $j'$. So Local fires on $Y=\Theta Y'$ with $\{\CI,\CIII\}$, with the orthogonal map $\Theta O'$.

By (ii) and (iii), and the relabelling of (i) back to $H$, some gluing makes Pair or Local fire on the configuration of Definition~\ref{def:glue} glued from the assignment. This is \lname{Tammes15.PaperSteps.killed_of_progKilled} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:dthree}]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:verdicts} it suffices that $\mathcal L$ satisfies the verdicts of the program. Let $P\in \mathcal L$ have $n$ vertices, let $k=15-n$, and let $H_0$ be the choice of hexagons of the program for a set of $k$ hexagons. The program meets its model (Definition~\ref{def:impl}(6), the assumption of Section~\ref{sec:notlean}), so its procedures are computed by the program in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:impl}(5). By Definition~\ref{def:impl}(6), the first level reads $P$ (Computation~\ref{comp:stageA}). If it rejects $P$, then by Definition~\ref{def:impl}(6) its narrowing in the first mode on the case of $H_0$ sends a box in the domain that contains the root box to empty, so the tree that is the root alone is accepted on $[\dlo,\dhi]$ in the first mode. Otherwise, by Definition~\ref{def:impl}(6), it writes $P$ to the survivors, and by Section~\ref{sec:replay} the replay reports $P$ verified on ranges of $d$ whose union contains the range of the program, which contains $[\dlo,\dhi]$ (Computation~\ref{comp:targets}); by Definition~\ref{def:impl}(6), on each of these ranges the procedures of the program accept a tree of the case of $H_0$ from a box in the domain that contains the root box. By Proposition~\ref{prop:sound}, the procedures of the program on the domain are sound and accept these trees, and Proposition~\ref{prop:soundtrees}, applied to a range that contains $d$, gives the verdicts of the program for every assignment with $d\in[\dlo,\dhi]$ that satisfies the relation system for $H_0$.
\end{proof}

\item[(D4)] $\mathcal F$ is not empty, and every $q\in\mathcal F$ consists of unit vectors, $\langle q_i,q_j\rangle=u$ with $i\ne j$ for $ij\in S_q$, and $\langle q_i,q_j\rangle\le u$ for all $i\ne j$.

\section{The bound of Fejes T\'oth}\label{sec:leanft}
This appendix proves the bound $d_{15}\le\dhi$ that the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} uses, by the route of the formalization. The proof of Fejes T\'oth \cite{FT43} bounds the area covered by caps about the points, no point of the sphere being covered more than twice. The formalization proves instead Proposition~\ref{prop:ftbound}: if some fifteen points of $\Sph$ are at pairwise distance at least $d$, then $d\le\dhi=56.6716^\circ$. This is weaker than the value $56.67150355^\circ$ of Fejes T\'oth, and it is all that the proof uses. The route is a variant of the first proof of \cite[Section~5.2, pp.~118 and~119]{FTK}, which triangulates the convex hull of the points and applies a triangle lemma to a face of smallest area: if the bound failed, the circumcentre of that face could be added to the points, again and again. Here the points are extended first to a saturated set at one level $c_0$, and every fan triangle of every facet of its hull is bounded at that level, as in the statement on saturated packings of \cite[Section~5.13, p.~155]{FTK}; the triangle lemma is stated at a fixed level rather than for the shortest side, and it is proved by polynomial identities rather than by Lexell's theorem and a figure. No area or measure enters. Put
\[
c_0=\frac{5494377}{10^7},\qquad \vartheta(c)=\arg\bigl(1+3c+i(1-c)\sqrt{1+2c}\bigr)\quad(1/2\le c<1).
\]

The triangle lemma computes angle sums by the following formula.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:anglesum}\leanmap{Tammes15.FejesToth.sphereVertexAngle_eq_arg, Tammes15.FejesToth.angle_sum_arg}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Let $p,q,r\in\Sph$ with $D=\langle p\times q,r\rangle>0$. The angle of the spherical triangle $pqr$ at $p$, between the arcs $pq$ and $pr$, is $\arg\bigl(\langle q,r\rangle-\langle p,q\rangle\langle p,r\rangle+iD\bigr)$.
\item Let $x,y,z<1$ and $D>0$ be real numbers with $D^2=1-x^2-y^2-z^2+2xyz$, and suppose that the arguments of $x-yz+iD$, $y-zx+iD$ and $z-xy+iD$ sum to more than $\pi$. Then they sum to $\pi+2\arg(1+x+y+z+iD)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(1) Put $x=\langle q,r\rangle$, $y=\langle r,p\rangle$ and $z=\langle p,q\rangle$. The tangent directions at $p$ towards $q$ and $r$ are proportional to $q-zp$ and $r-yp$, whose inner product is $x-yz$ and whose cross product is $D\,p$. So the angle at $p$ is $\arg(x-yz+iD)$.

(2) With the relation between $D$ and $x,y,z$,
\[
2(x-yz+iD)(y-zx+iD)(z-xy+iD)=-(1-x)(1-y)(1-z)\,(1+x+y+z+iD)^2 ,
\]
and $(1-x)(1-y)(1-z)>0$, so the sum of the three arguments equals $\pi+2\arg(1+x+y+z+iD)$ modulo $2\pi$. Both lie in $(\pi,3\pi)$: each of the three arguments lies in $(0,\pi)$, since $D>0$, and their sum exceeds $\pi$; and the argument of $1+x+y+z+iD$ lies in $(0,\pi)$. Both parts are proved in the formalization, (2) as \lname{Tammes15.FejesToth.angle_sum_arg}.
\end{proof}

With $x=y=z=c$ and $D=(1-c)\sqrt{1+2c}$, which satisfy the relation of (2), the three arguments are the angles of the equilateral triangle of side $\arccos c$, by (1), and by (2), the excess of a spherical triangle being positive, their sum is $\pi+2\vartheta(c)$. The next lemma says that a spherical triangle with sides at least $\arccos c$ and circumradius at most $\arccos c$ has at least this angle sum.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:fttri}\leanmap{Tammes15.FejesToth.fejesToth_triangle, Tammes15.FejesToth.gram_cross, Tammes15.FejesToth.circumcentre_identity, Tammes15.FejesToth.tri_sq_le, Tammes15.FejesToth.tri_poly, Tammes15.FejesToth.tri_mul_le, Tammes15.FejesToth.arg_le_arg_of_mul_le, Tammes15.inner_cross_cyc, Tammes15.Vendor.EM8.SquareAntiprismVerification.spherical_triangle_excess_positive}% english-ok: Lean declaration names
Let $1/2\le c<1$. Let $p,q,r\in\Sph$ with $\langle p\times q,r\rangle>0$ and $\langle p,q\rangle,\langle q,r\rangle,\langle r,p\rangle\le c$, and let $o\in\Sph$ and $\lambda\ge c$ satisfy $\langle o,p\rangle=\langle o,q\rangle=\langle o,r\rangle=\lambda$. Then the angles of the spherical triangle $pqr$ sum to at least $\pi+2\vartheta(c)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $x=\langle q,r\rangle$, $y=\langle r,p\rangle$, $z=\langle p,q\rangle$, $D=\langle p\times q,r\rangle$ and $s=1+x+y+z$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:anglesum}(1), applied to $(p,q,r)$, $(q,r,p)$ and $(r,p,q)$, which have the same $D$, the angles of the triangle are the arguments of $x-yz+iD$, $y-zx+iD$ and $z-xy+iD$. The Gram determinant of $p,q,r$ is $D^2=1-x^2-y^2-z^2+2xyz$, the numbers $x,y,z$ are at most $c<1$, and the three angles sum to more than $\pi$, the excess of a spherical triangle being positive. So by Lemma~\ref{lem:anglesum}(2) they sum to $\pi+2\arg(s+iD)$, and it suffices to show $\arg(s+iD)\ge\vartheta(c)$.

The inner products with $p$, $q$ and $r$ show that $D\,o=\lambda(p\times q+q\times r+r\times p)$, and $|p\times q+q\times r+r\times p|^2=D^2+2(1-x)(1-y)(1-z)$; hence $D^2(1-\lambda^2)=2\lambda^2(1-x)(1-y)(1-z)$, and since $\lambda\ge c>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:circ}
2c^2(1-x)(1-y)(1-z)\le(1-c^2)D^2 .
\end{equation}
For two numbers in the upper half plane, $\arg(s_0+iD_0)\le\arg(s+iD)$ when $s_0>0$ and $sD_0\le s_0D$. With $s_0=1+3c$ and $D_0=(1-c)\sqrt{1+2c}$ this holds if $s\le0$, and if $s>0$ it follows from $s^2(1-c)^2(1+2c)\le(1+3c)^2D^2$.

Put $m=1-c\in(0,1/2]$, $\xi_1=(c-x)/m$, $\xi_2=(c-y)/m$, $\xi_3=(c-z)/m$, all nonnegative, and $U_j=1+\xi_j$, so that $1-x=mU_1$, $1-y=mU_2$, $1-z=mU_3$. With
\[
H=2(U_1U_2+U_2U_3+U_3U_1)-U_1^2-U_2^2-U_3^2,\qquad P=U_1U_2U_3,\qquad Q=U_1+U_2+U_3,
\]
one has $D^2=m^2(H-2mP)$ and $s=4-mQ$, so that \eqref{eq:circ} is $m^3K\ge0$ with $K=(2-m)H-2P$, and the inequality to prove is $m^2F\ge0$ with
\[
F=(H-2mP)(4-3m)^2-(3-2m)(4-mQ)^2 .
\]
Let $e_1,e_2,e_3$ be the elementary symmetric functions of $\xi_1,\xi_2,\xi_3$, and $t_1=2-m$, $t_2=4-3m$. Then, identically,
\begin{align*}
F/2&=t_1^2e_1(t_2-t_1e_1)+t_2^2(t_1e_2-me_3),\\
F/2&=t_1^2K+t_1^2(t_1e_1-t_2)+t_1(5m^2-10m+4)e_2+(-9m^3+26m^2-24m+8)e_3,
\end{align*}
and $e_1e_2-9e_3=\xi_1(\xi_2-\xi_3)^2+\xi_2(\xi_3-\xi_1)^2+\xi_3(\xi_1-\xi_2)^2\ge0$. If $t_1e_1\le t_2$, both terms of the first form are nonnegative: $me_1\le mt_2/t_1<9t_1$, because $mt_2-9t_1^2=-12m^2+40m-36<0$, so $t_1e_2-me_3\ge e_2(9t_1-me_1)/9\ge0$. If $t_1e_1>t_2$, every term of the second form is nonnegative: $K\ge0$; $5m^2-10m+4=5(1-m)^2-1>0$ for $m\le1/2$; and $-9m^3+26m^2-24m+8=8(1-m)^3+m^2(2-m)>0$. This is \lname{Tammes15.FejesToth.fejesToth_triangle} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The triangle lemma, summed over the fan triangles of the facets of a hull, gives the bound.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ftbound}\leanmap{Tammes15.fejesToth_bound, Tammes15.FejesToth.N1, Tammes15.FejesToth.N2, Tammes15.FejesToth.exists_saturated, Tammes15.FejesToth.sep_card_bound, Tammes15.FejesToth.hB_of_sat, Tammes15.FejesToth.facetPolar_norm_lt, Tammes15.FejesToth.facet_fan_bound, Tammes15.FejesToth.hull_fan_count, Tammes15.hullGraph, Tammes15.exists_angular, Tammes15.facetPolar, Tammes15.isFacetPolar_facetPolar, Tammes15.facetPolar_strict, Tammes15.hull_corner_total, Tammes15.hull_euler}\mapnote{of type \texttt{Tammes15.FejesTothBound}}
If some fifteen points of $\Sph$ are at pairwise distance at least $d$, then $d\le\dhi$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $x_1,\dots,x_{15}\in\Sph$ be at pairwise distance at least $d$, and suppose $d>\dhi$.

(1) Since $\cos\dhi\le c_0$ (N1 below) and $\cos$ decreases on $[0,\pi]$, $\langle x_i,x_j\rangle<c_0$ for $i\ne j$; in particular the $x_i$ are distinct.

(2) A set of unit vectors with pairwise inner products at most $c_0$ has boundedly many points: finitely many balls of radius $\sqrt{2-2c_0}/2$ cover $\Sph$, by compactness, and two points in one ball have inner product greater than $c_0$. So among the finite sets of unit vectors that contain $x_1,\dots,x_{15}$ and have pairwise inner products at most $c_0$ there is one of largest size, $Y$. It is \emph{saturated}: every $z\in\Sph$ has $\langle z,y\rangle>c_0$ for some $y\in Y$, since otherwise $Y\cup\{z\}$ would be a larger such set. As $c_0>0$, no closed hemisphere contains $Y$, and $|Y|\ge15$.

(3) Lemma~\ref{lem:hull} applies to $Y$, a finite set of distinct unit vectors in no closed hemisphere. Each facet $f$ of its graph of exposed pairs is a cycle through $v_f\ge3$ distinct points of $Y$, which lie on a plane $\langle\cdot,N_f\rangle=1$ (scaling $N_f$), while $\langle y,N_f\rangle\le1$ for every $y\in Y$, and every vertex of $f$ other than the ends of a side lies strictly on the left of that side. The corners at each point of $Y$ sum to $2\pi$, and with $E$ edges and $F$ facets, $|Y|-E+F=2$.

(4) Let $o_f=N_f/|N_f|$ and $\lambda_f=1/|N_f|$. Then $\langle o_f,y\rangle\le\lambda_f$ for every $y\in Y$, with equality at the vertices of $f$, and saturation at $z=o_f$ gives $\lambda_f>c_0$.

(5) The facet $f$ is a convex spherical polygon in the sense of the eight-point proof \cite{KLT}, whose angles are the corners of the rotation system. The diagonals from its first vertex cut it into $v_f-2$ fan triangles whose angles add up to those of $f$. Each fan triangle satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:fttri} with $c=c_0$, $o=o_f$ and $\lambda=\lambda_f$: its vertices are distinct points of $Y$, and it is positively oriented by the last property of step (3). So the angles of $f$ sum to at least $(v_f-2)(\pi+2\vartheta(c_0))$.

(6) Summing over the facets, with $\sum_fv_f=2E$ and the corner sums of step (3), gives $2\pi|Y|\ge(2E-2F)(\pi+2\vartheta(c_0))=(2|Y|-4)(\pi+2\vartheta(c_0))$, that is $(2|Y|-4)\,2\vartheta(c_0)\le4\pi$.

(7) Since $|Y|\ge15$, this gives $52\,\vartheta(c_0)\le4\pi$, against $\vartheta(c_0)>\pi/13$ (N2 below). This is \lname{Tammes15.fejesToth_bound} in the formalization.
\end{proof}

The two numerical facts are proved in Lean:
\begin{itemize}
\item[N1.] $\cos\bigl(566716\,\pi/1800000\bigr)\le c_0$, that is $\cos\dhi\le c_0$;
\item[N2.] $\pi/13<\vartheta(c_0)$, from $9709417579/10^{10}\le\cos(\pi/13)$ and $\vartheta(c_0)=\arccos\bigl((1+3c_0)/\sqrt{2(1+c_0)^3}\bigr)$, where $(1+3c_0)^2<2(1+c_0)^3(9709417579/10^{10})^2$ is a comparison of rationals.
\end{itemize}
Both cosines are bounded by Taylor polynomials with remainder bounds, with $3.1415926535\le\pi\le3.1415926536$ from the bounds on $\pi$ in Mathlib.
